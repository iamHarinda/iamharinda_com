A website has scroll space. A brochure has multiple pages. A corporate polo shirt has, realistically, a few square inches on the chest and maybe a larger back panel, a genuinely small, fixed canvas that still has to represent your brand clearly, at a glance, from a normal conversational distance. That constraint shapes the design in some pretty specific ways.

## Simplify before you shrink anything

A logo with fine detail, small text, or subtle gradients that reads perfectly on a business card or website often loses that detail entirely once it's reduced to embroidery or small-scale print size. Rather than shrinking a complex logo and hoping it holds up, a simplified version, built specifically for small-scale reproduction, usually reads far more clearly at the actual size it'll be seen at in real life.

## Decide what actually needs to be on the shirt

Not every brand element needs to appear on the polo. A full logo lockup with a tagline, a wordmark, and a symbol might work fine on a letterhead, but a polo often reads better with just the symbol or a simplified mark. The full identity doesn't need to get crammed into a three-inch chest placement just because it exists elsewhere.

## Colour contrast against the actual garment

A logo that works on a white background can lose all contrast on navy, and the reverse holds true too. For a corporate order, this often means having a version of the logo specifically adapted for the garment colour being used, not just placing the standard logo file as-is and hoping it happens to work.

## Consistency across a range of garment colours

If your company orders polos in more than one colour, which is common, different colours for different roles or seasons, the design needs a plan for how it adapts across all of them, not just the one colour it was originally designed against and approved for.

## Where the back panel actually fits in

A corporate order commonly uses the back panel for a larger, secondary element, a tagline, a larger version of the mark, sometimes a simple pattern that reinforces brand colours without needing to say anything specific at all. It's a genuinely different design job than the chest placement, even on the same garment, and it benefits from being considered on its own rather than just scaled up from the chest version.

## The overall goal worth keeping in mind

A corporate polo design succeeds when someone can recognize the brand instantly, from a normal distance, without needing to read fine print or squint at detail that was never going to survive the reduction to garment scale in the first place.

## A tip for ordering across departments

If different departments or roles need slightly different placements, note that clearly upfront. It's much easier to build variations from one solid base design than to retrofit them later after everything's already approved.
