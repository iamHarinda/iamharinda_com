Shopping for a monitor for photo editing turns into a wall of numbers fast: Delta E, sRGB coverage, panel type, refresh rate, response time. Most of that list matters for gaming, not editing, and figuring out which specs actually affect your work versus which ones are just marketing noise saves both money and confusion.

## The specs that genuinely matter for editing

Delta E, a measure of how far off a colour reads from its true value, is the single most important number on the spec sheet. Anything under 2 is considered accurate enough for professional work, and a lot of consumer monitors don't get anywhere close without calibration. sRGB coverage matters next, since that's the colour space most web delivery and most client viewing happens in. Aim for at least 99% coverage, ideally paired with strong Adobe RGB or DCI-P3 coverage if you also do print work or video. Panel type affects consistency, not just sharpness. IPS and IPS Black panels hold colour and contrast steady across a wider viewing angle than the cheaper TN or basic VA panels still common at lower price points.

## Specs that matter less than you'd think

Refresh rate, the number that gaming monitors compete hardest on, barely matters for editing work at all. A 60Hz panel edits a photo exactly as well as a 240Hz one, since you're not tracking fast motion. Response time, another gaming-focused spec, falls into the same category. Don't pay extra for either unless you're also using the same monitor for something that actually needs them.

## Resolution and size, together

A 27-inch monitor at 4K resolution gives you enough pixel density to see fine detail, skin texture, sharpness, subtle banding, without needing to zoom in constantly. A 32-inch 4K panel gives more working room for multiple windows side by side but spreads the same pixel count over more physical area, so detail reads slightly less sharp per inch. Neither is wrong; it comes down to your desk space and whether you prefer a bigger canvas or a denser one.

## Why a monitor needs to actually be calibratable

Some budget monitors, particularly ones aimed at general office use, don't respond well to hardware calibration, holding onto their factory settings more stubbornly than higher-end panels built with colour-accurate work in mind. Before buying, it's worth checking whether a specific model is commonly used and calibrated successfully by other photographers, rather than assuming any monitor can be corrected equally well.

## Connectivity that actually simplifies your workflow

USB-C connectivity with 65 watts or more of power delivery lets a single cable both drive the display and charge a connected laptop, which cleans up a desk considerably compared to juggling a separate power brick and video cable. This matters more than it sounds like it should once you're plugging and unplugging a laptop from a docked setup daily.

## A realistic budget range

A genuinely capable editing monitor meeting the specs above doesn't require spending thousands. Solid options exist in the four hundred to eight hundred dollar range that hit Delta E under 2, full sRGB coverage, and a good IPS panel. Spending more buys you things like wider gamut coverage for print work, higher resolution, or a larger screen, but the baseline accuracy needed for confident editing decisions is available well below the premium end of the market.

## What actually happens if you skip this and use a laptop screen

Laptop screens, even good ones, are usually smaller, dimmer, and less accurate out of the box than a dedicated external monitor, and they're rarely calibrated by default. Editing exclusively on a laptop screen isn't a disaster, but pairing it with an external monitor for serious colour work removes a real source of guesswork from every session.

## The order to actually buy things in

If budget is tight, prioritize a monitor that meets the accuracy specs above, then add a colorimeter to calibrate it, before spending on anything else in your setup, a faster computer, extra storage, a nicer chair. None of those other upgrades fix inaccurate colour, and inaccurate colour is the thing most likely to show up as a visible problem in delivered work.
