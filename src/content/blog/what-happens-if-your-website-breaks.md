At some point, most websites have a moment where something stops working — a page won't load, a form stops sending, an image disappears. It's rarely a disaster, but the panic of "I don't know who's supposed to fix this or what it'll cost" is a genuinely common and avoidable problem.

## The most common reasons a site breaks

- **An expired domain or SSL certificate** — both need periodic renewal, and a missed renewal is one of the most common causes of a site suddenly going down entirely.
- **A plugin or software update conflict**, mostly a WordPress-specific issue — one plugin updates, and it doesn't play well with another.
- **Hosting issues** — an outage, a resource limit hit, or a hosting account that lapsed on payment.
- **A change made without testing** — content or design edits that accidentally break something else on the page.

## Who's actually responsible

This depends entirely on what was agreed at launch, which is exactly why it should be agreed at launch rather than discovered afterward:

- **If you have an ongoing maintenance agreement**, the developer or agency handling it is responsible, usually within a defined response time.
- **If you don't**, you're generally on your own to either fix it (for simple things — a login issue, a content mistake) or pay for one-off support at whatever the developer's hourly or emergency rate is.
- **If the site runs on a platform you manage yourself** (a builder, or WordPress you administer), platform-level outages are usually the platform's responsibility, but anything specific to your site's setup is yours.

## What a reasonable support arrangement looks like

- A defined period after launch (commonly 30 days) where fixes related to the original build are included at no extra charge.
- A clear, separate rate for ongoing maintenance or emergency fixes after that — ideally quoted upfront, not discovered mid-crisis.
- A realistic response time stated in writing, not just implied.

## What to do right now if you don't know the answer

If you currently have a website and genuinely don't know who would fix it if it broke tomorrow, that's worth resolving before it happens rather than after — ask whoever built it directly, or get a second opinion from someone else who can look at the setup and tell you honestly what your actual coverage is.

## The cost of not knowing

A broken site during business hours, with no clear plan for who fixes it, often means it stays broken for days while you scramble to find someone — during which you're invisible to anyone searching for you or trying to reach you online. A clear plan, agreed in advance, is what turns that into a same-day fix instead.
