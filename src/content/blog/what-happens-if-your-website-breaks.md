At some point, most websites have a moment where something just stops working. A page won't load, a form stops sending, an image disappears overnight for no obvious reason. It's rarely a disaster, but the panic of not knowing who's supposed to fix it, or what it'll cost, is a genuinely common and completely avoidable problem.

## The most common reasons a site actually breaks

An expired domain or SSL certificate. Both need periodic renewal, and a missed one is one of the most common causes of a site suddenly going down entirely, seemingly out of nowhere. A plugin or software update conflict, mostly a WordPress-specific headache, where one plugin updates and doesn't play well with another that hasn't caught up yet. Hosting issues, an outage, a resource limit getting hit, or a hosting account that lapsed on payment without anyone noticing in time. A change made without testing, content or design edits that accidentally break something else on the page nobody was looking at.

## Who's actually responsible when it happens

This depends entirely on what got agreed at launch, which is exactly why it should be agreed at launch rather than discovered the hard way afterward. If you have an ongoing maintenance agreement, the developer or agency handling it is responsible, usually within a defined response time written somewhere. If you don't have one, you're generally on your own to either fix it yourself, for simple things, a login issue, a content mistake, or pay for one-off support at whatever the developer's hourly or emergency rate happens to be. If the site runs on a platform you manage yourself, a builder, or WordPress you administer, platform-level outages are usually the platform's responsibility, but anything specific to your own site's setup is on you.

## What a reasonable support arrangement actually looks like

A defined period after launch, commonly thirty days, where fixes related to the original build are included at no extra charge. A clear, separate rate for ongoing maintenance or emergency fixes after that window closes, ideally quoted upfront rather than discovered mid-crisis. A realistic response time stated in writing somewhere, not just implied or assumed.

## What to do right now if you don't know the answer

If you currently have a website and genuinely don't know who would fix it if it broke tomorrow, that's worth resolving before it actually happens rather than after. Ask whoever built it directly, or get a second opinion from someone else who can look at the setup and tell you honestly where you actually stand right now.

## The real cost of not knowing this

A broken site during business hours, with no clear plan for who fixes it, often means it stays broken for days while you scramble to find someone, and during that stretch you're invisible to anyone searching for you or trying to reach you online. A clear plan, agreed on in advance, is what turns that into a same-day fix instead of a multi-day scramble.
