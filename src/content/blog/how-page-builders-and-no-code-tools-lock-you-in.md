Page builders and no-code platforms get sold on freedom. Build it yourself, no developer needed, change anything anytime you want. What's less advertised is how difficult some of them make it to actually leave once you've built something real on top of them.

## What "lock-in" actually means in practice

Lock-in is when switching away from a platform costs more, in time, money, or lost work, than it reasonably should, given that you're the one who created the content in the first place. It shows up in a few specific, recognizable ways. Your content doesn't export cleanly. Many builders store pages in a proprietary format that doesn't translate into a standard website file, meaning "exporting" your site often just means starting over on a new platform, not actually moving what you built. Your design doesn't transfer either. Even when export is technically possible, the layout, styling, and functionality built with a specific builder's tools rarely recreate themselves accurately anywhere else. Your domain and content get tangled with the platform account itself. Some builders make it genuinely hard to fully separate your business's identity from their platform's ecosystem, even after you've paid to use it for years.

## Why platforms get built this way on purpose

It's not necessarily malicious. It's a reasonable business incentive from their side. A platform that's easy to leave has to keep earning your subscription through genuine value every single month. One that's hard to leave keeps your subscription through switching costs instead, whether you're happy or not. Both models exist in the market right now, and it's worth knowing which one you're actually signing up for before you build three years of content on top of it.

## How to check before you commit to any platform

Search "[platform name] export website" and see what actual export options exist. A genuine HTML export is a very different thing from "you can copy your text into a new site manually, one field at a time." Read what happens to your domain if you cancel. Does the site simply go down, or is there a real transition path laid out somewhere? Look at whether the platform allows any custom code at all. Even limited code injection is a sign the platform isn't trying to keep everything fully proprietary and locked down.

## Why custom-coded sites don't have this problem

A custom-coded site is just files, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, that belong to you outright and run on any standard hosting anywhere. There's no proprietary format to get locked into, no platform account your business's website secretly depends on continuing to exist. Moving hosting providers, changing developers, or handing the project to someone new is a normal, unremarkable process, not a migration project that eats a month of your time.

## The honest tradeoff here

Custom development costs more upfront specifically because there's no shared platform absorbing part of that cost across millions of other users. What you're buying with that extra cost includes never having to solve a lock-in problem later. For a business planning to stick around for years, that's often worth more than it first appears when you're just comparing sticker prices.

No platform lock-in, no proprietary format, the full source code transfers to you at launch, hosted wherever you choose to put it.
