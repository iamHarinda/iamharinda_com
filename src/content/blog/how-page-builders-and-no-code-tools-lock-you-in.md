Page builders and no-code platforms are sold on freedom — build it yourself, no developer needed, change anything anytime. What's less advertised is how difficult some of them make it to actually leave once you've built something real on top of them.

## What "lock-in" actually means

Lock-in is when switching away from a platform costs more — in time, money, or lost work — than it reasonably should, given that you're the one who created the content in the first place. It shows up in a few specific ways:

- **Your content doesn't export cleanly.** Many builders store pages in a proprietary format that doesn't translate to a standard website file — meaning "exporting" your site often means starting over on a new platform, not actually moving what you built.
- **Your design doesn't transfer.** Even when export is technically possible, the layout, styling and functionality built using a specific builder's tools rarely recreates itself accurately anywhere else.
- **Your domain and content are tangled with the platform account.** Some builders make it hard to fully decouple your business's identity from their platform's ecosystem, even after you've paid to use it for years.

## Why platforms are built this way

It's not necessarily malicious — it's a reasonable business incentive. A platform that's easy to leave has to keep earning your subscription through genuine value every month; one that's hard to leave keeps your subscription through switching costs instead. Both models exist in the market, and it's worth knowing which one you're signing up for.

## How to check before you commit to a platform

- Search "[platform name] export website" and see what actual export options exist — a genuine HTML export is very different from "you can copy your text into a new site manually."
- Read what happens to your domain if you cancel — does the site simply go down, or is there a real transition path?
- Look at whether the platform allows any custom code — even limited code injection is a sign the platform isn't trying to keep everything fully proprietary.

## Why custom-coded sites don't have this problem

A custom-coded site is just files — HTML, CSS, JavaScript — that belong to you outright and run on any standard hosting. There's no proprietary format to be locked into, no platform account your business's website depends on continuing to exist. Moving hosting providers, changing developers, or handing the project to someone new is a normal, unremarkable process rather than a migration project.

## The honest tradeoff

Custom development costs more upfront specifically because there's no shared platform absorbing part of that cost across millions of other users. What you're buying with that extra cost includes never having to solve a lock-in problem later — for a business planning to stick around, that's often worth more than it first appears.
