Ask most wedding photographers where their editing time actually goes, and culling — not colour correction, not retouching — is usually the honest answer. Deciding what stays and what goes across 2,000–3,500 frames from a single wedding day is slow precisely because every decision feels like it matters.

## Why culling drags on

Two things make culling slower than it needs to be: reviewing images out of order (jumping between similar shots instead of comparing them directly), and re-deciding on frames that were already effectively rejected the first time through. A structured pass system fixes both.

## A three-pass system

**Pass 1 — Fast reject, no thinking.** Go through the full shoot at speed, flagging only the obvious rejects: eyes closed, motion blur, test shots, duplicates from a burst where nothing changed. Don't stop to compare or second-guess — this pass exists purely to remove the easy no's, typically 30–40% of a wedding shoot.

**Pass 2 — Compare near-duplicates.** For remaining burst sequences and near-identical frames, use a side-by-side compare view rather than flipping between them one at a time. Pick the sharpest, best-expression frame from each burst and reject the rest. This is where most of the real judgment happens.

**Pass 3 — Final gallery pass.** Review only what survived passes one and two, this time thinking about gallery composition — do you have enough variety (wide shots, details, candids, posed), and is anything essential missing (rings, dress detail, both families) even if the individual shot isn't a standout.

## Rules that keep the process fast

- **Decide once.** Don't revisit a pass-1 reject during pass 2 "just to check." If it was rejected on the fast pass, trust that decision.
- **Cull on a second monitor or a fast machine.** Culling thousands of RAW previews on hardware that lags between frames adds real time across a whole shoot.
- **Set a target gallery size before starting.** Knowing you're aiming for roughly 400–600 final images (a common range for a full wedding day) gives pass three a concrete goal instead of an open-ended "keep whatever's good."
- **Cull before editing, always.** Colour-correcting or retouching a photo that gets cut in a later pass is wasted time — cut first, edit only what survives.

## Where AI culling tools fit

AI-assisted culling tools (built into Lightroom and available as standalone apps) speed up pass one specifically — flagging closed eyes, blur and duplicates automatically. They're a reasonable time-saver for that mechanical first pass. The comparison judgment in pass two and the gallery-composition thinking in pass three are still where a person's eye matters, since neither is really about detecting technical flaws — they're about which photo tells the story better.
