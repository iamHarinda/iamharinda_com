Ask most wedding photographers where their editing time actually goes, and culling, not colour correction, not retouching, is usually the honest answer. Deciding what stays and what gets cut across two to three and a half thousand frames from one wedding day is slow precisely because every single decision feels like it matters.

## Why culling drags on longer than it needs to

Two things make culling slower than necessary. Reviewing images out of order, jumping between similar shots instead of comparing them directly, and re-deciding on frames that were already effectively rejected the first time through. A structured pass system fixes both problems at once.

## Run it in three passes

Pass one is a fast reject with no thinking involved. Go through the full shoot at speed, flagging only the obvious rejects, eyes closed, motion blur, test shots, duplicates from a burst where absolutely nothing changed between frames. Don't stop to compare or second-guess anything here. This pass exists purely to clear out the easy no's, usually thirty to forty percent of a wedding shoot disappears in this one pass alone.

Pass two is where you compare near-duplicates. For remaining burst sequences and near-identical frames, use a side-by-side compare view instead of flipping between them one at a time. Pick the sharpest, best-expression frame from each burst and reject the rest without hesitation. This is where most of the real judgment actually happens.

Pass three is your final gallery pass. Review only what survived the first two passes, but this time think about gallery composition. Do you have enough variety, wide shots, details, candids, posed moments, and is anything essential missing, rings, dress detail, both families together, even if the individual shot on its own isn't a standout.

## Rules that keep the whole process moving

Decide once. Don't revisit a pass-one reject during pass two "just to check." If it got rejected on the fast pass, trust that call and move on.

Cull on a second monitor or a fast machine if you can. Culling thousands of RAW previews on hardware that lags between every frame adds up to real time across a whole shoot, more than people expect until they time it.

Set a target gallery size before you even start. Knowing you're aiming for roughly four hundred to six hundred final images, a common range for a full wedding day, gives pass three a concrete goal instead of an open-ended "keep whatever looks good" approach that never actually ends.

Cull before you edit, always, no exceptions. Colour-correcting or retouching a photo that gets cut in a later pass is wasted time you'll never get back.

## Where AI culling tools actually help

AI-assisted culling tools, built into Lightroom and available as standalone apps now, speed up pass one specifically, flagging closed eyes, blur, and duplicates automatically without you having to look at each one. They're a genuinely reasonable time-saver for that mechanical first pass. The comparison judgment in pass two and the gallery-composition thinking in pass three still need a person's eye, since neither is really about spotting technical flaws. They're about which photo actually tells the story better, and that's a judgment call software still can't make reliably.
