Most viewers can't name why a wedding photo looks slightly wrong — they just sense it. Almost every case traces back to one of a small set of specific editing choices, all more common than they should be.

## 1. Skin pushed too warm or too orange

The single most common issue. It usually comes from correcting a cool colour cast too aggressively, or from a warm-toned preset applied without checking against actual skin. The fix is checking skin tone against a neutral reference, not against how "warm and inviting" the image feels on its own — warmth that looks pleasant in isolation can still be objectively wrong.

## 2. Over-smoothed skin

Covered in more depth elsewhere, but worth repeating: blur-based smoothing that removes texture along with blemishes is the fastest way to make real skin look like a rendering. If pores and fine texture are gone at 100% zoom, the retouch has gone too far, even if it looks fine at a glance.

## 3. Crushed shadows or blown highlights from an aggressive contrast preset

A punchy, high-contrast look is popular, but pushed too far it clips real detail out of the darkest and brightest parts of the image — a groom's dark suit becomes a solid black shape with no fabric detail, a bright window becomes a flat white void. Contrast should add punch without deleting information that was actually recoverable.

## 4. Inconsistent white balance across a gallery

A gallery where every third photo has a slightly different colour cast reads as unfinished, even if no single photo looks obviously wrong on its own. This usually comes from correcting images individually without a shared reference point, or syncing settings across images that didn't share the same lighting to begin with.

## 5. Oversaturated colour, especially greens and skin-adjacent tones

Boosting global saturation to make an image feel more vivid tends to hit skin tones and foliage hardest, since they already sit at moderate-to-high saturation before any adjustment. The result: skin that looks sunburnt, grass that looks radioactive. Selective saturation adjustments (by colour range, not global) avoid this.

## 6. Sharpening applied too heavily

Aggressive sharpening creates visible haloing around high-contrast edges — a bright rim around dark hair against a light background, for example — and exaggerates skin texture in a way that looks harsh rather than crisp. Sharpening should be checked at 100% zoom specifically for halos before finalizing.

## 7. A grade that ignores the lighting it's applied to

A cool, moody grade built for outdoor daylight, applied without adjustment to a warm indoor reception shot, fights the base lighting instead of working with it — producing a muddy, indecisive colour result rather than the intended mood.

## What ties all of these together

Every mistake on this list comes from applying an adjustment — a preset, a batch sync, a global slider — without checking it against the specific image it's being applied to. None of them are hard to avoid; they're just easy to miss without a deliberate check at 100% zoom against a neutral reference, image by image.
