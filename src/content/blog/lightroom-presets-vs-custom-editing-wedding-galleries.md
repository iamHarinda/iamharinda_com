Presets get sold on one promise: apply a whole look in one click instead of building it from nothing. For a single photo, sure, that's genuinely true. For a full wedding gallery shot across a day of shifting light, the real math gets a lot more complicated than the marketing suggests.

## What presets are actually good at

A preset is just a saved set of Develop adjustments, a starting point, not a finished edit no matter how polished it looks in a demo. They're genuinely useful for giving a gallery a consistent starting look before you do the per-image correction. They speed up personal or lower-stakes work where a fast turnaround matters more than pixel-perfect accuracy. They're also a solid reference for photographers still building their own editing style, since seeing a finished look broken into its parts teaches you something you can't get from just admiring a finished photo.

## Where the time savings quietly vanish

A preset built for one lighting condition, applied across a full wedding day of shifting light, needs correcting on almost every frame it doesn't match. And that's most of the frames, on a day that moves from an indoor getting-ready room to an outdoor ceremony to a dim reception hall. At that point you're not skipping the correction step. You're doing it after the preset instead of before it, and that's often slower than starting from neutral in the first place.

There's another cost too. Presets apply a fixed formula by definition, which means skin gets treated exactly the same way the preset treats everything else sitting in that tonal range. A preset that gives a gorgeous teal-and-orange grade on a sunset portrait can push that same shift onto skin under different lighting, because it has no idea it's looking at a person rather than a background element.

## Custom editing takes longer, but the gallery holds together

Editing from a neutral starting point, correcting white balance and exposure per image first, then layering a consistent creative grade on top, takes more time per photo than a single click. But skin tones get judged against how they actually look in that specific frame, not forced through a fixed formula built for someone else's photos. The grade ends up intentional, adjusted for the real lighting condition instead of fighting against it. And the finished gallery holds together as one set, because every image got corrected toward a common target rather than pushed through identical settings regardless of where it started from.

## A reasonable middle ground worth trying

Most efficient studios build their own custom preset out of their own past edits, not a purchased one, as a starting point that's genuinely close to their real style. Then they hand-correct per lighting group on top of that. That's a different thing entirely from applying an off-the-shelf preset and hoping it fits. It's closer to a personal shortcut for repeating decisions you've already made, not a replacement for making them in the first place.

If you're outsourcing a gallery rather than editing it in-house, this is worth asking about directly. Is the editor running one preset formula across the whole batch, or correcting and grading by eye, photo by photo? That answer tells you a lot about how consistent the skin tones will end up looking across the finished set.

## How to actually test a preset before trusting it with a real gallery

Before committing a full wedding to any preset, whether it's one you built or one you bought, run it across ten photos spanning the widest range of lighting from a recent shoot, bright outdoor, dim indoor, mixed reception light. If the preset needs heavy correction on more than a couple of those ten, it's not saving you the time it promised. This small test takes fifteen minutes and tells you more about a preset's real-world usefulness than any amount of reading a description or watching a demo video ever will.

## What purchased preset packs rarely tell you upfront

Preset packs sold online are almost always built and demonstrated on a specific type of photo, often a bright, evenly lit outdoor portrait shot on a specific camera system. That's the best-case scenario the preset was designed around, and it's rarely representative of a full wedding day's actual range of lighting. This is why so many photographers buy a preset pack, love it on the demo images, and then find it needs heavy adjustment on their own indoor reception shots. Knowing this upfront saves the disappointment of assuming a preset failed you when it was really just never tested against the conditions you actually shoot in.

## The honest takeaway

Neither approach is wrong on its own. Presets save real time on lower-stakes work and speed up a first pass on big galleries. Custom editing protects the details that actually matter on the photos people will keep forever. Most studios that survive peak season use some blend of both, and knowing which one you're actually paying for matters more than the marketing language either side uses.

## What clients actually notice, versus what photographers obsess over

It's worth being honest that most wedding clients can't tell the difference between a well-applied preset and a fully custom hand edit at a casual glance, especially scrolling through a gallery on a phone. What they do notice, reliably, is inconsistency, one photo looking warm while the next looks cool, or skin tones that shift oddly from image to image. This means the real value of custom editing isn't that it looks dramatically better than a good preset on any single photo. It's that it's far less likely to produce the kind of inconsistency that clients actually pick up on without being able to explain why something feels off about their gallery.

## A practical hybrid worth considering seriously

Rather than treating this as an all-or-nothing choice, plenty of photographers apply a custom preset as the very first step on every photo in a lighting group, then still go through and hand-correct exposure and any problem areas individually. This isn't really "using a preset" in the sense of trusting it blindly. It's using a preset as a faster starting point for work you were going to do by hand anyway, which shaves real time off the process without reintroducing the consistency risk that comes from trusting a preset to handle the whole job on its own.
