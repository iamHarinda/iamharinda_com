Presets are sold on one promise: apply a look in one click instead of building it from scratch. For a single photo, that's true. For a full wedding gallery shot across changing light all day, the real math is more complicated.

## What presets are actually good at

A preset is a saved set of Develop adjustments — a starting point, not a finished edit. They're genuinely useful for:

- Giving a gallery a consistent starting look before per-image correction.
- Speeding up personal or low-stakes work where perfect accuracy matters less than a fast turnaround.
- Photographers still developing their own editing style, as a reference for what a finished look can involve.

## Where the time savings disappear

A preset built for one lighting condition applied across a full day of shifting light needs correcting on almost every frame it doesn't match — which is most of them, on a wedding day that moves from an indoor getting-ready room to an outdoor ceremony to a dim reception. At that point you're not skipping the correction step, you're doing it after the preset instead of before it, which is often slower than just correcting from a neutral starting point.

The other cost: presets by definition apply a fixed formula, which means skin tones get treated the same way a preset treats everything else in that tonal range. A preset that gives a beautiful teal-and-orange grade on a sunset portrait can push the same shift onto skin in a different lighting scenario, because it doesn't know it's looking at skin.

## Custom editing: slower per photo, more consistent per gallery

Editing from a neutral starting point — correct white balance and exposure first, per image, then apply a consistent creative grade on top — takes longer per photo than one click. But it means:

- Skin tones get judged against what they actually look like in that frame, not against a fixed formula.
- The grade is intentional and adjusted for the actual lighting condition, not fought against it.
- The final gallery holds together as a set, because every image was corrected to a common target rather than pushed through the same fixed adjustment regardless of its starting point.

## A reasonable middle ground

Most efficient studios build their own custom preset from their own past edits — not a purchased one — as a starting point that's genuinely close to their real style, then hand-correct per lighting group on top of it. That's different from applying an off-the-shelf preset and hoping it fits: it's closer to a personal shortcut for repeating your own decisions, not a substitute for making them.

If a gallery is being outsourced rather than edited in-house, this is worth asking about directly: is the editor applying one preset formula across the batch, or correcting and grading by eye, image by image? That answer predicts a lot about how consistent the skin tones will look across the finished set.
