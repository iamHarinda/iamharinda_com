Almost everyone who's had a bad experience getting a website built can point to a warning sign they noticed and talked themselves out of. None of these require technical knowledge to spot — they're about how someone communicates and structures the work, not about code.

## 1. No written scope before payment

If a developer asks for money before putting the actual scope — pages, features, price, timeline — in writing, that's the single biggest red flag on this list. A written scope is what protects both sides from disagreements later about what was actually promised.

## 2. Vague or shifting pricing

"It depends" is a fair answer before scope is discussed. After a scope conversation, a legitimate developer should be able to give you a real number, or a clearly bounded range with the reasons for the range explained. Pricing that stays vague even after you've explained exactly what you need is worth questioning.

## 3. No examples of past work

Anyone who's built real websites for real clients should be able to show you some, ideally with a way to verify they actually built it (not just a screenshot). No portfolio, or a portfolio you can't verify, isn't automatically disqualifying for someone new to the field — but it does mean you're taking on more risk than usual.

## 4. Unwillingness to commit to code and domain ownership

Covered in more depth elsewhere, but it's worth restating as a red flag on its own: if a developer is vague or resistant about handing over full code access and domain ownership at launch, that's worth pressing on before you commit, not after.

## 5. No plan for what happens after launch

If nobody can tell you who fixes something if it breaks two months from now, or what ongoing maintenance actually costs, you're finding out for the first time exactly when you're least prepared to negotiate — mid-crisis, with a broken site.

## 6. Pressure to decide immediately

A legitimate freelancer or agency wants you to make an informed decision, because a client who understood what they were buying is a better long-term client. Heavy pressure to sign quickly, or discounts that expire within hours, are sales tactics more common in less reputable operations.

## 7. Payment structured entirely upfront

Some upfront payment (a deposit) is normal and reasonable. 100% upfront, with nothing tied to delivery or milestones, removes your only real leverage if the work doesn't go as promised.

## What good looks like, for comparison

A written proposal before any payment. A clear, fixed price once scope is set. Real, verifiable past work. A stated plan for ownership transfer and post-launch support. No pressure to decide on the spot. None of this is unusual to ask for — a developer who bristles at reasonable questions like these is telling you something useful before you've spent anything.
