Almost everyone who's had a bad experience getting a website built can point to a warning sign they noticed and talked themselves out of at the time. None of these require technical knowledge to spot either. They're about how someone communicates and structures the work, not about code at all.

## No written scope before any payment

If a developer asks for money before putting the actual scope, pages, features, price, timeline, in writing, that's the single biggest red flag on this whole list. A written scope is what protects both sides from disagreements later about what was actually promised versus what someone remembers being said on a call.

## Vague or shifting pricing

"It depends" is a fair answer before scope gets discussed. After that conversation happens, a legitimate developer should be able to give you a real number, or a clearly bounded range with the reasons for that range explained plainly. Pricing that stays vague even after you've explained exactly what you need is worth questioning directly.

## No examples of past work anywhere

Anyone who's built real websites for real clients should be able to show you some, ideally with a way to actually verify they built it and not just a screenshot pulled from somewhere else. No portfolio, or a portfolio you can't verify, isn't automatically disqualifying for someone newer to the field, but it does mean you're taking on more risk than usual going in.

## Unwillingness to commit to code and domain ownership

Covered in more depth elsewhere, but worth restating as its own red flag. If a developer is vague or resistant about handing over full code access and domain ownership at launch, that's worth pressing on before you commit to anything, not after the project's already underway.

## No plan for what happens after launch

If nobody can tell you who fixes something if it breaks two months from now, or what ongoing maintenance actually costs, you're finding this out for the first time exactly when you're least prepared to negotiate, mid-crisis, with a site that's currently down.

## Pressure to decide immediately

A legitimate freelancer or agency wants you making an informed decision, because a client who actually understood what they were buying tends to be a better long-term client. Heavy pressure to sign quickly, or discounts that expire within hours, are sales tactics more common in less reputable operations than solid ones.

## Payment structured entirely upfront

Some upfront payment, a deposit, is normal and reasonable practice. A hundred percent upfront, with nothing tied to actual delivery or milestones, removes your only real leverage if the work doesn't go the way you were promised it would.

## What good actually looks like, for comparison

A written proposal before any payment changes hands. A clear, fixed price once scope is set in stone. Real, verifiable past work you can actually check. A stated plan for ownership transfer and post-launch support. No pressure to decide on the spot, ever. None of this is unusual to ask for, and a developer who bristles at reasonable questions like these is telling you something useful before you've spent a dollar.
