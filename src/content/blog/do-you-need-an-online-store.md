Should your site let people buy directly, or just let them get in touch first? It's worth answering honestly before a project starts, because full e-commerce is a meaningfully bigger, more expensive build than a site that simply presents what you offer and lets someone reach out to talk.

## What a "contact me" site actually covers

A site built around inquiries, a contact form, a phone number, a booking link, maybe pricing shown as a starting range, works well for service-based businesses where every job looks a bit different, or where you want a real conversation before committing a customer to a firm price. Most consultants, photographers, contractors, and custom-work businesses fall into this category, even if they technically sell something in the end.

## What a genuine online store adds on top

An online store needs a product catalog with variations, size, colour, quantity, a shopping cart, secure payment processing, inventory tracking, shipping calculation, and usually order management with automatic email confirmations too. Each of those is a real feature, not just a checkbox to tick, and that's exactly why e-commerce sites typically cost more and take longer to build than a comparable informational site sitting next to it.

## A simple test to figure out which one you need

Ask yourself this. Does a customer's purchase depend on a conversation, a custom quote, or scheduling, or is it a fixed product at a fixed price, ready to buy the moment someone decides they want it? If it's the first, a contact-based site with clear pricing information is often genuinely better for conversion, not just cheaper. Forcing a service business into a rigid checkout flow can create friction instead of removing it. If it's the second, physical products, fixed-price digital downloads, prepaid packages, an actual store is the right tool for the job.

## A middle ground worth knowing about

For businesses selling a small number of fixed-price things, an online course, a handful of physical products, a package booking, without needing a full catalog, a simpler payment integration, a "buy now" button connected to a payment processor, can cover the need without the cost and complexity of a full e-commerce build sitting behind it. It's worth asking about specifically if a full store feels like overkill for what you actually sell.

## What this means for your budget

A basic informational or contact-driven site starts in the low thousands. A genuine e-commerce build, with product management and checkout, is a meaningfully larger project, both in the upfront cost and in what it takes to maintain afterward, inventory, order fulfillment, payment processor fees stacking up over time. Being clear about which one you actually need, before requesting quotes, keeps you from either overpaying for capability you'll never use or underbuilding something that can't do what your business genuinely needs.

## A last honest point

Plenty of service businesses add a store later, once they've validated demand for a specific fixed-price offer. There's nothing wrong with starting simple and expanding once you actually know what people want to buy.
