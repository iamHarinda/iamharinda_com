"I need a website" is the start of a conversation, not really enough for anyone to quote accurately off of without a lot of follow-up questions first. The good news is that describing what you actually need doesn't require technical language at all. It just needs a bit of structure, and here's a template for building that structure yourself before that first call.

## What the site is actually for

Not the pages, the purpose behind them. Is it mainly to bring in new customers who find you searching online for what you offer? To give existing customers a place to book or pay conveniently? To act as a professional-looking reference point people check before deciding whether to work with you at all? This single sentence shapes almost everything else about the project that follows from it.

## Who's actually going to visit it

Local customers searching nearby for exactly your service? People referred by word of mouth checking you out before reaching out directly themselves? Other businesses, if you're B2B rather than consumer-facing? Knowing the audience shapes tone, content, and sometimes even which features matter most to include. A booking calendar makes sense for direct consumers booking appointments. A case-study-heavy site makes more sense for B2B relationships built on trust and proof.

## What it needs to actually do

Beyond "look good," list the actual functions you need it to perform. A contact form, a way to show pricing clearly, a booking calendar, a blog you'll update yourself, a way for people to pay online directly, a gallery of past work to browse. Being specific here, even in totally plain language, is what turns a vague quote into an accurate one you can actually trust and budget around.

## What you'll manage yourself versus hand off

Will you be updating content regularly, or is this closer to "build it once and it mostly stays the same for years without much change"? This single answer often determines whether you need a CMS at all, which changes both the cost and the build time involved significantly.

## Examples you actually like

Two or three websites, any industry at all, that you like the feel of, with a note on what specifically appeals to you about each one you've chosen. This communicates far more precisely than trying to describe a design in the abstract, no matter how good you are with words normally.

## Your rough budget and timeline

Even an approximate range, under two thousand dollars, need it live within a month, helps a developer propose something realistic instead of guessing at what you can actually work with financially and time-wise.

## What this looks like all put together

"I run a small bakery and want a site that brings in local customers searching for custom cakes in our area. Most visitors will be nearby people checking us out before calling us directly. I need a way to show our cake gallery, a contact form, and pricing info clearly laid out. I'll want to add new photos myself every month or so as we make new designs. I like the feel of these two sites, especially how simple they are to navigate around. Budget is flexible up to about two thousand dollars, and I'd like it live within six weeks if possible."

That's a complete, non-technical brief a developer can quote accurately from, no jargon required anywhere in it, just genuine clarity about what you actually want built.

## Why writing this down first helps even before the call

Putting this into words, even roughly and imperfectly, before your first conversation with a developer often clarifies your own thinking about the project. Business owners frequently discover gaps or contradictions in their own vision simply by trying to write it out clearly, catching issues before they become confusing mid-project surprises that cost time to untangle later.

## What happens after you share this with a developer

Once a developer has this information, they can usually turn it around into a fixed-price proposal within a day or two, sometimes faster for a straightforward project like a simple marketing site. From there, the conversation shifts from figuring out what you want to actually planning how to build it, which is a much more productive place to spend everyone's time and energy than the back-and-forth guessing that happens without a clear brief in hand from the start.

## A brief isn't a permanent contract

Nothing about writing this down locks you into every detail forever without room to change your mind. A good developer treats your initial brief as a starting point for conversation, not a rigid checklist, and will ask clarifying questions or suggest adjustments based on their own experience with similar projects they've built before for other clients in similar situations to yours.

## What happens when scope isn't clarified upfront

Projects that start without this kind of clarity tend to run into trouble partway through, a developer builds based on assumptions that turn out to be wrong, or a client realizes halfway through that they actually wanted something different from what was originally described. Both situations cost real time and sometimes real money to correct, which is exactly why investing a bit of effort into a clear scope before work begins pays for itself many times over across the life of the project.

## A last encouragement for anyone feeling stuck

If none of this feels natural to write out on your own, that's completely normal and not a sign you're doing anything wrong. Plenty of business owners find it easier to talk through their idea out loud on a call than to type it up in advance, and a good developer knows how to draw out the right details through simple conversation rather than expecting a perfectly polished brief from day one, before you've even had a chance to think it through properly.
