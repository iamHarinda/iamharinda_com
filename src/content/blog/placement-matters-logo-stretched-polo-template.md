You can spot it immediately even without knowing why. A polo shirt with a logo that just looks slightly off, stretched, oddly positioned, sitting in a way that doesn't quite match the shirt underneath it in a way that's hard to name. It's one of the most common signs of design done without real attention to the garment itself, and it's genuinely avoidable once you know what actually causes it in the first place.

## Stretching to fill a template

A common shortcut is taking a logo built for a different shape, a square social media avatar, a wide horizontal wordmark, and simply stretching it to fill a mockup template's placement area without adjusting the proportions properly. This distorts the logo's actual proportions, and it's often visible even to someone with zero design training looking at the finished result. The logo just looks "off" in a way that's hard to name precisely but easy to notice at a glance from across a room.

## Ignoring the placket and collar

A polo's placket, the buttoned front strip, and its collar interrupt the flat printable area in a way a t-shirt doesn't have to account for at all in its own design process. A design placed as if the chest area were a flat, uninterrupted rectangle can end up looking crowded near the collar, or positioned in a way that looks unbalanced once the shirt's actually being worn, folded collar, visible buttons, fabric with natural drape instead of lying perfectly flat like it does on a screen.

## Wrong scale relative to the body

A design that looks appropriately sized on a flat mockup, viewed close-up on a screen during the design process, can read as too large or too small once it's actually on a person standing at a normal viewing distance in a real setting. Scale needs judging against how the garment will actually be seen, worn, at a normal distance, not just how it looks zoomed in on a design file sitting on a monitor during review.

## Not accounting for fabric drape and texture

A flat digital mockup shows a design on a perfectly smooth surface with no real-world imperfections. Real fabric has texture, seams, and drape that a flat render doesn't fully capture in most standard mockup tools. This is one reason a genuinely useful mockup uses a realistic 3D-rendered or photographed garment template, not a flat outline with the design just pasted on top of it without any consideration for how fabric actually behaves.

## What good placement actually looks like

The design sits naturally within the available flat area, respects the collar and placket rather than crowding them awkwardly, is scaled to look balanced at a normal viewing distance on a worn garment, and gets checked against a realistic mockup, not a flat template, before anyone approves it for production.

## A simple test anyone can run before approving a design

Print the mockup at actual size, or view it on a screen held at arm's length, roughly the distance someone would stand from you in conversation, and see if the design still reads clearly and feels balanced. This quick, low-tech check catches scale and placement issues that are easy to miss when a design file fills an entire monitor during the review process.

## Why this is worth checking before you approve anything

A placement problem is far easier and cheaper to fix at the mockup stage than after a production run's already happened and boxes of shirts are sitting in a warehouse waiting to be distributed. Always ask to see the design on a realistic garment mockup, and genuinely look at whether it feels naturally placed, not just whether the logo itself looks correct sitting in isolation on its own away from the actual garment.

## Common excuses that let placement problems slip through

"It's close enough" and "nobody will notice" are two phrases worth watching for during any review process, since they're usually a sign time pressure is starting to override genuine quality judgment. A placement issue that feels minor in a rushed final review often becomes the one thing a customer or team member actually points out once the shirts arrive, precisely because they're seeing it fresh without the fatigue of having stared at the same mockup for hours already.

## How this problem often starts before design even begins

Placement problems frequently trace back to a logo built without ever considering it would end up on apparel at all, designed purely for a website header or a business card with proportions that make sense in those specific contexts. Recognizing this early, and being willing to build a genuinely apparel-adapted version rather than forcing the original file to work everywhere, prevents the stretching and distortion problem before it even has a chance to happen during placement.

## What to do if you're evaluating a design someone else created

If you're reviewing a polo design built by a previous designer or an in-house team member without much apparel experience, checking specifically for these placement issues, stretching, ignoring the collar and placket, wrong scale, gives you a clear, objective way to assess quality even without any design background yourself. It turns a vague "something feels off" into specific, actionable feedback you can actually communicate clearly to whoever's doing the revision work, which tends to get a faster, more accurate fix than a general complaint alone would.

## A final reminder before signing off on any design

Placement issues are almost always fixable at little to no cost when caught early, and almost always expensive once a production run is already underway. That asymmetry alone justifies the extra few minutes it takes to check a mockup properly before giving final approval on any order, large or small, rushed or not, urgent deadline or plenty of time to spare before the order needs to ship.

Every design here gets shown on a realistic front and back mockup before it's finalized, never a flat template with artwork just pasted on top.
