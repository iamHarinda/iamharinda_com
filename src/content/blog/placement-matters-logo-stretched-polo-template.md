You can spot it immediately even without knowing why: a polo shirt with a logo that just looks slightly off — stretched, oddly positioned, sitting in a way that doesn't quite match the shirt underneath it. It's one of the most common signs of design done without real attention to the garment itself, and it's avoidable once you know what causes it.

## Stretching to fill a template

A common shortcut is taking a logo built for a different shape — a square social media avatar, a wide horizontal wordmark — and simply stretching it to fill a mockup template's placement area. This distorts the logo's actual proportions, which is often visible even to someone with no design training: the logo just looks "off" in a way that's hard to name precisely.

## Ignoring the placket and collar

A polo's placket (the buttoned front strip) and collar interrupt the flat printable area in a way a t-shirt doesn't have to account for. A design placed as if the chest area were a flat, uninterrupted rectangle can end up looking crowded near the collar, or positioned in a way that looks unbalanced once the shirt is actually being worn — folded collar, buttons visible, fabric with natural drape rather than lying perfectly flat.

## Wrong scale relative to the body

A design that looks appropriately sized on a flat mockup, viewed close-up on a screen, can read as too large or too small once it's actually on a person standing at a normal viewing distance. Scale needs to be judged against how the garment will actually be seen — worn, at a normal distance — not just how it looks zoomed in on a design file.

## Not accounting for fabric drape and texture

A flat digital mockup shows a design on a perfectly smooth surface. Real fabric has texture, seams, and drape that a flat render doesn't fully capture — which is one reason a genuinely useful mockup uses a realistic 3D-rendered or photographed garment template, not a flat outline with the design pasted on top.

## What good placement actually looks like

The design sits naturally within the available flat area, respects the collar and placket rather than crowding them, is scaled to look balanced at a normal viewing distance on a worn garment, and is checked against a realistic mockup — not a flat template — before it's approved.

## Why this is worth checking before you approve any design

A placement problem is far easier and cheaper to fix at the mockup stage than after a production run has already happened. Always ask to see the design on a realistic garment mockup, and genuinely look at whether it feels naturally placed — not just whether the logo itself looks correct in isolation.
