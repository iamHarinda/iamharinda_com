If you've ordered design work and been told your files are "vector," or asked whether you need "the vector version," and weren't entirely sure why it mattered, it's a genuinely important distinction for anything headed to print, embroidery, or reproduction at more than one size.

## The actual difference between the two

A raster image, JPEG, PNG, most photos you've ever taken, is built from a fixed grid of pixels, a specific number of coloured dots at a specific resolution. Zoom in far enough, or scale it up past its original size, and those individual pixels become visible as blocky, blurry edges. A vector image, built in software like Illustrator and saved as Ai, EPS, or a vector PDF, is built from mathematical paths and shapes instead, lines and curves defined by equations, not fixed dots on a grid. A vector image can scale to any size at all, a business card or a billboard, with perfectly crisp edges every single time, no exceptions.

## Why this specifically matters for apparel and pattern design

A polo shirt design might need to appear at several different sizes, a small embroidered version on a left chest, a larger printed version on a back panel, possibly resized again for a hang tag or packaging down the line. A raster file created at one size doesn't scale cleanly to a much larger size without visible quality loss showing up. A vector file scales to any of these needs from the exact same original artwork, with no quality loss at all, no matter how big or small it ends up.

It also matters directly for production itself. Many printing and embroidery digitizing processes work from vector files specifically, because the clean paths translate more accurately into the machine instructions those processes actually use. A raster file often needs to get redrawn as vector artwork before certain production methods can use it at all.

## When raster is actually completely fine

A final mockup image, a photo-realistic preview showing the design on the actual garment, is normally delivered as a raster image, JPEG or PNG, since it's meant to be viewed, not scaled or reproduced through another production process. Social media posts, website images, anything purely for viewing, are reasonably fine as raster. The distinction really matters specifically for files headed to a printer, embroiderer, or supplier down the line.

## What to check you're actually getting

If you're ordering design work meant to go into production, confirm you're receiving an editable vector source file, Ai or vector PDF, not just flattened JPEG or PNG exports handed to you as the final product. A designer who can only hand over raster files either isn't working in vector to begin with, or isn't including the source file. Either way, it's worth asking about directly before you commit to an order.

## A quick way to spot the difference yourself

Open the file and zoom way in. If edges stay crisp no matter how far you zoom, it's vector. If they turn blocky and pixelated, it's raster. Takes about ten seconds and settles the question completely.

Every design here includes the editable Ai vector source file, alongside PDF, PNG and JPG, not just flattened images handed over at the end.
