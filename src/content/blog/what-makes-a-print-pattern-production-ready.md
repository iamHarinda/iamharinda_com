One of the more frustrating experiences in ordering apparel design is getting artwork back that looks great, sending it off to a printer or supplier, and having it rejected or flagged as unusable. Not because the design is bad, but because it isn't actually set up correctly for production in the first place.

## Vector, not just a flat image

Most professional printing methods, screen printing, embroidery digitizing, large-format sublimation, need artwork in vector format, built from mathematical paths rather than a fixed grid of pixels, so it can scale to any size without losing quality along the way. A design that only exists as a flattened JPEG or PNG, especially one at a small original size, often can't scale up cleanly for a full print run without visible quality loss showing up.

## Correct colour mode

Screens display colour in RGB. Most physical printing works in CMYK, a different colour model with a smaller range of achievable colours than what you see on screen. Artwork designed and approved only in RGB can shift noticeably once it's converted for CMYK printing, a bright, saturated colour on screen can come out duller or just plain different in print. Production-ready artwork accounts for this from the start, rather than leaving colour conversion as an afterthought nobody thought about.

## Resolution that holds up at actual print size

An image that looks sharp at the small size you're viewing it at on screen can look pixelated or blurry once printed at the actual size it'll appear on a garment. Production-ready files get built or exported at a resolution suited to their real physical output size, not just "big enough to look fine in a quick preview."

## Correctly separated elements, for methods that need it

Screen printing in particular often needs colours separated into distinct layers, or spot colours, since each colour typically requires its own screen to print. A design that looks fine as a single flattened image can need genuine rework if the colours weren't kept separate and adjustable during the actual design process.

## Clean, simplified vector paths

Overly complex vector artwork, thousands of tiny anchor points, unnecessarily intricate detail, can cause real problems for embroidery digitizing especially, where fine detail below a certain size simply can't get reproduced in stitching no matter how good the machine is. Production-ready design accounts for the limitations of the actual production method, not just how the artwork happens to look as a flat image on a screen.

## Why this matters for who you actually hire

A designer who only produces polished-looking flat images without accounting for the real production method is handing you a design that looks finished but might need genuine rework before a supplier can even use it. Ask specifically whether the files you'll receive are set up for the printing or embroidery method you're actually using. That answer matters more than how good the preview mockup looks sitting in your inbox.

## A simple check before you send anything to a printer

Ask your printer directly what format and colour mode they need before final files get built, not after. A five-minute question upfront saves a full round of rework later.

Every order here comes with Ai, PDF, PNG and JPG files at print resolution, the formats a print shop or supplier can actually run with directly.
