One of the more frustrating experiences in ordering apparel design is getting artwork back that looks great, sending it to a printer or supplier, and having it rejected or flagged as unusable — not because the design is bad, but because it isn't actually set up correctly for production. Here's what that step covers.

## Vector, not just a flat image

Most professional printing methods (screen printing, embroidery digitizing, large-format sublimation) need artwork in vector format — built from mathematical paths rather than a fixed grid of pixels — so it can be scaled to any size without losing quality. A design that only exists as a flattened JPEG or PNG, especially at a small original size, often can't be scaled up cleanly for a full print run without visible quality loss.

## Correct colour mode

Screens display colour in RGB; most physical printing works in CMYK, a different colour model with a smaller range of achievable colours. Artwork designed and approved only in RGB can shift noticeably in colour once converted for CMYK printing — a bright, saturated colour on screen can come out duller or slightly different in print. Production-ready artwork accounts for this from the start, rather than leaving colour conversion as an afterthought.

## Resolution that holds up at actual print size

An image that looks sharp at the small size you're viewing it on screen can look pixelated or blurry once printed at the actual size it'll appear on a garment. Production-ready files are built or exported at a resolution suited to their actual physical output size, not just "big enough to look fine in a preview."

## Correctly separated elements, for methods that need it

Screen printing in particular often needs colours separated into distinct layers (spot colours), since each colour typically requires its own screen. A design that looks fine as a single flattened image can need real rework if the colours weren't kept separate and adjustable during the design process.

## Clean, simplified vector paths

Overly complex vector artwork — thousands of tiny anchor points, unnecessarily intricate detail — can cause real problems for embroidery digitizing especially, where fine detail below a certain size simply can't be reproduced in stitching. Production-ready design accounts for the limitations of the actual production method, not just how the artwork looks as a flat image.

## Why this matters for who you hire

A designer who only produces polished-looking flat images without accounting for the actual production method is handing you a design that looks finished but may need real rework before a supplier can use it. Ask specifically whether the files you'll receive are set up for the printing or embroidery method you're actually using — that answer matters more than how good the preview mockup looks.
