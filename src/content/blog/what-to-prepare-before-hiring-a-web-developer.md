The vaguer the brief, the vaguer the quote — and the more likely a project runs into scope surprises halfway through. None of what a developer needs upfront is technical; it's just organized. Here's what actually helps.

## 1. A clear list of pages you need

Not a technical spec — just a plain list: Home, About, Services, Pricing, Contact, whatever applies to your business. If you're not sure, "a site that explains what I do and lets people reach me" is a fine starting point; a developer can help shape it from there.

## 2. Your actual content, or a plan to get it

You don't need finished copy written before the first call, but knowing whether you'll write it yourself, need help writing it, or already have most of it ready changes both the timeline and sometimes the price. The single biggest cause of delayed projects is waiting on content that was assumed to be "almost ready."

## 3. Examples of sites you like (and don't)

Two or three sites — even from completely different industries — that you like the feel of, plus a note on what specifically you like (the layout, the colours, how simple it feels to navigate). This communicates far more accurately than trying to describe a design in words alone.

## 4. Your logo and brand basics, if you have them

A logo file, brand colours, fonts if you have a preference. If you don't have any of this yet, say so upfront — it's a normal starting point, and it just means a design direction gets established as part of the project rather than assumed already settled.

## 5. What the site actually needs to do

Beyond "look professional" — does it need a contact form, a booking calendar, an online store, a blog you'll update yourself? This is the difference between a $750 landing page and a $4,000 web application, and it's worth being specific about even if you're not sure of the technical term for what you want.

## 6. A rough budget range

You don't need an exact number, but even a rough range ("under $2,000" vs. "flexible if it does what I need") helps a developer propose something realistic instead of guessing and potentially wasting both your time.

## 7. Your domain, if you already have one

If you've already registered a domain, have the login details ready, or at least know which registrar it's with. If you haven't yet, that's fine too — it's usually set up as part of the project.

## Why this preparation actually saves money

A vague brief forces a developer to either guess (and risk quoting wrong) or spend extra time clarifying scope before they can quote at all. A clear brief, even an imperfect one, gets you a faster, more accurate quote — and a smoother project once it starts.
