The vaguer the brief, the vaguer the quote, and the more likely a project runs into scope surprises halfway through the build. None of what a developer needs from you upfront is technical. It's just organized, and most of it takes an afternoon to pull together.

## A clear list of pages you need

Not a technical spec, just a plain list. Home, About, Services, Pricing, Contact, whatever actually applies to your business. If you're not sure yet, "a site that explains what I do and lets people reach me" is a totally fine starting point. A developer can help shape it further from there.

## Your actual content, or at least a plan to get it

You don't need finished copy written before the first call, but knowing whether you'll write it yourself, need help writing it, or already have most of it ready changes both the timeline and sometimes the price. The single biggest cause of delayed projects is waiting on content everyone assumed was "almost ready" when it really wasn't.

## Examples of sites you like, and ones you don't

Two or three sites, even from completely different industries, that you like the feel of, plus a note on what specifically you like about each one, the layout, the colours, how simple it feels to navigate around. This communicates far more accurately than trying to describe a design purely in words.

## Your logo and brand basics, if you already have them

A logo file, brand colours, fonts if you have a preference either way. If you don't have any of this yet, just say so upfront. It's a completely normal starting point, and it just means a design direction gets established as part of the project rather than assumed already settled beforehand.

## What the site actually needs to do

Beyond just "look professional," does it need a contact form, a booking calendar, an online store, a blog you'll update yourself regularly? This is the real difference between a seven hundred fifty dollar landing page and a four thousand dollar web application, and it's worth being specific about even if you're not sure of the exact technical term for what you want.

## A rough budget range

You don't need an exact number here, but even a rough range, under two thousand dollars versus flexible if it does what I need, helps a developer propose something realistic instead of guessing and possibly wasting both your time on a quote that never lands.

## Your domain, if you already have one

If you've already registered a domain, have the login details ready, or at least know which registrar it's with. If you haven't yet, that's fine too. It's usually set up as part of the project itself.

## Why this preparation actually saves you money

A vague brief forces a developer to either guess, and risk quoting wrong, or spend extra time clarifying scope before they can even quote at all. A clear brief, even an imperfect one, gets you a faster, more accurate quote, and a smoother project once the actual work starts.
