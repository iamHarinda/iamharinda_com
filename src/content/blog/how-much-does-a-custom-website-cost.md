Ask three people what a website costs and you'll get three wildly different numbers — and all of them can be technically correct, because "a website" covers everything from a $200 DIY template to a $30,000 custom web application. The number only makes sense once you know what's actually being built.

## The rough price bands

- **DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace, etc.):** Roughly $100–$500 a year, mostly subscription fees. You build it yourself using a drag-and-drop editor.
- **WordPress with a freelancer:** Typically $2,000–$8,000 for a professionally designed site on top of WordPress, plus ongoing hosting and plugin costs.
- **Fully custom-coded site:** Generally $3,000–$15,000+ depending on page count and complexity, built from scratch rather than on a pre-made platform.
- **Custom web applications** (booking systems, client portals, anything beyond a marketing site): Often $4,000 and up, priced around the specific functionality needed.

## What actually moves the price within each band

- **Number of pages and how much content needs writing or organizing**, not just designing.
- **Whether you need to edit it yourself afterward** — a site wired up with a content system you can update is more work upfront than a static one only a developer can change.
- **Custom functionality** — a booking calendar, a client login, a payment system — costs more than a site that's purely informational.
- **Design from scratch vs. a template** — a fully original design takes longer than adapting an existing layout.

## Why "custom" often costs more than a builder, and why that's sometimes worth it

A builder is cheaper because you're doing the assembly yourself inside a pre-made system, and that system is shared across millions of other sites — it can't be tailored past a certain point. Custom development costs more because someone is building your specific site, with your specific structure, rather than fitting your business into a template built for everyone. Whether that's worth the difference depends on how much you actually need something a template can't do — and for a lot of small businesses, especially at first, it genuinely isn't necessary.

## What's often missing from the initial quote

Ask specifically about: hosting (where the site actually lives — not included in a one-time build price), a domain name (usually $10–$20/year separately), ongoing maintenance (security updates, backups), and whether revisions beyond the first draft cost extra. A quote that only covers the build, with no mention of what happens after launch, is an incomplete picture of the real cost.

## A more useful question than "how much"

"What am I getting for this price, and what happens after launch if something needs to change?" gets you a much more useful answer than the number alone — two very different sites can cost the same amount, and two very similar sites can cost wildly different amounts depending on what's included.
