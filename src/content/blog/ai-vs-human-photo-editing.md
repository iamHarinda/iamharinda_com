Every wedding photographer hits the same fork in the road eventually. Keep editing every gallery by hand, or start handing some of it to AI. Neither choice is wrong on its own. The trick is knowing which photos should go where.

Aftershoot, Imagen, and tools like them have gotten genuinely good. Feed one a few thousand RAW files from a Saturday wedding and it'll churn out consistent exposure and color across the whole set in maybe twenty minutes. That used to take a person a full day. For a photographer booking forty weddings a year, that time back is real money.

But AI edits from patterns. It doesn't understand why a bride's dress needs to stay true white instead of drifting warm under church lighting. It can't tell you which twenty photos out of a four-hundred-image gallery are the ones a couple will actually print and hang on their wall. It just applies what it's learned across the whole batch and moves on.

## Where AI genuinely earns its keep

AI shines with volume. A big gallery, hundreds or thousands of shots, needs a first pass of basic corrections and it needs it fast. If the lighting stays fairly consistent, one venue, similar conditions throughout, AI handles that first pass well. Speed matters more than a hand-checked result on every single frame here, and that's exactly the tradeoff AI is built for.

Think about a photographer shooting three weddings in one weekend. There's no realistic way to hand-edit all of that before Monday. AI closes that gap. It's not cutting corners so much as it's doing the job a person physically can't finish in time.

## Where a human editor still wins

Judgment calls are where AI falls apart. Mixed lighting, a groom's skin tone reading wrong against a stone wall, a gallery where every image needs to feel like it belongs next to the others, these need a person looking closely, not an algorithm guessing at averages.

A small number of hero images in every gallery deserve that closer look regardless of how the rest gets handled. The first kiss. The ring shot. The photo that ends up in a frame on someone's mantle for the next thirty years. Those shouldn't get the same treatment as a hallway shot nobody will look at twice.

Studios that promise a hand-crafted look have to actually deliver one all the way through. If your marketing says "no presets, no filters, every photo edited by a real person," your delivery process needs to match that promise, not quietly cut against it because a deadline snuck up.

## The blend most studios actually run

Here's what's become pretty normal by 2026: nobody picks a side anymore. A photographer runs the full gallery through AI or a bulk editing service for the first colour pass, then pulls the shots that matter most, engagement portraits, family formals, the first dance, and sends those to a human editor for a proper look. Costs stay reasonable and the images that get printed still get real attention.

If you're the type who edits everything from RAW by eye on a calibrated screen and skips presets entirely, that's a completely fine choice too. It just means your pricing is built around photo count rather than editing speed, and every single frame gets looked at rather than batch-processed through an algorithm.

## Three questions before you outsource a shoot

Ask yourself these before deciding how to handle a gallery:

How many photos are we talking about, and how similar is the lighting across them? Big, consistent sets are where AI saves the most real time.

Which images will your client actually print or frame? Those deserve a hand edit no matter how the rest of the gallery gets processed.

Does your brand promise something specific and hand-crafted? If your marketing leans on "no AI, no shortcuts," your delivery has to back that up, all the way to the last file.

There's no single right answer buried in these questions. There's just a mismatch worth avoiding. The photographers who move fastest through outsourcing are the ones matching the editing method to what each gallery actually needs instead of picking one approach and forcing everything through it.
