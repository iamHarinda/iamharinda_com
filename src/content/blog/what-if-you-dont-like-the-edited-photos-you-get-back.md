This is the question sitting underneath almost every hesitation about outsourcing editing. You send off RAW files you can't reshoot, and what if what comes back just isn't right? It's a completely reasonable fear, and the answer depends entirely on what kind of service you're actually working with.

## What a legitimate service does when this happens

A properly structured editing service builds revisions into the price from the start, meaning "I don't like this" isn't a dead end, it's the beginning of a normal conversation. You point out specifically what feels off, too warm, too flat, missing detail in a certain area, and the editor adjusts and sends it back. This continues until it's right, not until you've used up some limited number of allowed changes.

## Why specificity matters when giving feedback

"I don't like it" alone doesn't give an editor much to work with. "The skin tones feel too orange, and the shadows in the reception shots feel too dark" gives someone something concrete to actually fix. The more specific your feedback, the faster a revision round closes the gap between what you got and what you actually wanted.

## What separates a bad outcome from a fixable one

A photo that's technically flawed, blown highlights, a colour cast, poor sharpening, is almost always fixable through revision, since the underlying RAW data still holds the information needed to correct it. A photo that simply doesn't match your personal style preference is also fixable, but it requires clearer reference material upfront next time, since taste is harder to correct after the fact than a technical error.

## Why this fear is worse in theory than in practice

Most photographers who actually try outsourcing for the first time describe the first gallery as a calibration round more than a finished product, expecting some back and forth rather than a perfect result on the first try. Once an editor understands your style through that first round, subsequent galleries typically need far fewer adjustments, since the reference point is now well established.

## What to do before you ever reach this point

Sending reference edits and a written style note upfront, warm but not orange skin, whites should stay bright, dramatically reduces how often you end up disliking a first-pass result at all. Most dissatisfaction traces back to insufficient direction going in, not an editor's actual skill or effort.

## A red flag worth watching for

If a service charges extra for every revision round, or caps revisions at one or two before additional fees kick in, that changes the actual risk calculation considerably. A service confident in matching your style typically doesn't need to limit how many times you can ask for something to be adjusted, since getting it right the first few times builds the same trust a limited-revision model tries to protect against losing.

## What happens in the rare case it genuinely doesn't work out

If after multiple honest attempts a specific service still isn't matching your style, that's useful information, not a personal failure on your part. Not every editor fits every photographer's aesthetic, and recognizing a mismatch early, before committing a full season, protects both your time and theirs.

## How to word feedback so it actually gets acted on well

Rather than a long list of vague impressions, structure feedback around specific images and specific issues, "photo 14, the shadows need to open up a bit" is far more actionable than "some of these feel dark." Editors work fastest and most accurately from concrete, image-specific notes rather than broad emotional reactions to a whole gallery at once.

## What a reasonable number of revision rounds actually looks like

Most galleries settle within two or three rounds of feedback once a working relationship with an editor is established, sometimes fewer once your style is well documented through references. If a gallery is still noticeably off after several genuine attempts with specific feedback each time, that's worth a direct conversation about whether the fit is right, rather than continuing to iterate indefinitely without real progress.

## Why this fear shouldn't stop you from testing the option at all

The photographers who avoid outsourcing entirely because of this exact worry often end up more constrained by their own workload than they'd be by a slightly imperfect first-round edit that's simply fixable through the normal revision process every legitimate service already builds in.
