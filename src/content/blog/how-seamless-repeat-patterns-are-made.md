If you're ordering a repeat pattern rather than designing one yourself, you don't need to know the software. But understanding the actual process makes it a lot easier to brief a designer well and know exactly what you're approving at each stage along the way.

## Step one, the concept

Before any tiling happens at all, the individual motifs get designed, the actual elements that will repeat, a floral shape, a geometric icon, a golf motif, whatever fits your brief. This stage is about the art itself, not yet about how it'll eventually repeat.

## Step two, arranging the tile

The individual elements get arranged inside a single square or rectangular tile, positioned so the pattern will flow correctly once it repeats, nothing too clustered in one spot, nothing that creates an obvious line where the tile edges meet up.

## Step three, testing the repeat

This is the step most people never see, and it's where most of the real craftsmanship actually happens. The tile gets previewed repeated multiple times in a grid, checked for visible seams, awkward gaps, or elements that accidentally line up in a way that creates an unintended shape or line running across the repeat.

## Step four, adjusting based on that test

Almost no first attempt is perfect, and that's completely normal. Elements get nudged, resized, or recoloured based on what the tiled preview reveals, often several rounds of this before the repeat actually reads as seamless rather than just technically tiled on paper.

## Step five, colour and finishing

Once the repeat itself is solid, colour variations, shading, and fine details get finalized. Sometimes multiple colourways of the same pattern get built at this stage too, since it's far easier to adjust colour on an already-working tile than to rebuild the whole repeat for each new variant.

## Step six, export for production

The finished pattern gets exported in whatever formats a printer or supplier actually needs, typically a vector file, editable and infinitely scalable, plus flattened image formats at production resolution, sized correctly for the specific printing method being used down the line.

## What this actually means for you as the person ordering

Knowing this process means you can ask specifically to see the tiled preview from step three or four, not just the single motif on its own. And you know that revision requests at that stage, "this feels too busy in the top corner," "the spacing looks uneven right here," are completely normal and part of actually getting a repeat pattern right, not a sign anything went wrong the first time around.

## Why skipping steps causes real problems later

Rushing straight from concept to export, skipping the repeat testing entirely, is the single most common reason a finished pattern looks fine as one tile and wrong once it's actually printed across a whole garment. If a designer offers to skip that testing step to save time, it's worth asking them not to.
