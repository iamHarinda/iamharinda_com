Not every printing method handles colour the same way, and the method you're planning to use should shape the design before it's finalized — not get discovered as a constraint after the artwork is already done.

## Screen printing: fewer colours, by design

Screen printing works by pushing ink through a separate stencil (screen) for each individual colour in the design — meaning every additional colour adds real cost and production complexity, since each one needs its own screen and pass through the press. Screen-printed designs are typically kept to a limited palette (often somewhere between one and six colours) specifically because of this cost structure, and patterns designed for screen printing generally need to work within that limitation from the start, using flat, distinct colour areas rather than gradients or subtle blending.

## Sublimation: effectively unlimited colour

Sublimation printing works completely differently — it's a dye-transfer process that can reproduce full-colour, photo-realistic designs with gradients, shading and essentially unlimited colours, since it's not building up ink layer by layer through separate screens. This is why sublimation is the standard choice for the kind of complex, all-over, richly coloured patterns increasingly common on golf and performance apparel — designs that would be prohibitively expensive or simply impossible with screen printing's colour-by-colour cost structure.

## Embroidery: a different constraint entirely

Embroidery isn't printing at all — it's thread, stitched in place — and it has its own limitations: very fine detail and tiny text don't translate well into stitching, and while embroidery can technically use many thread colours, each one adds real machine time and cost. Embroidered logos are typically simplified compared to a printed version of the same design, specifically to work within what stitching can accurately reproduce.

## Why this needs to be decided before design, not after

A pattern designed freely with rich gradients and dozens of subtle colour variations, intended for screen printing, either needs a real redesign to reduce it to a workable colour count, or needs to switch to sublimation to be reproduced as designed. Knowing your production method upfront lets a designer build the pattern correctly the first time, rather than discovering a mismatch after the design is already approved.

## What to tell a designer before you order

Simply stating the intended production method — "this will be screen printed," "this is going through sublimation," "this needs to work as an embroidered logo" — is enough for a designer to make the right calls on colour count and detail level from the very first concept, instead of building something that needs reworking once it hits an actual production quote.
