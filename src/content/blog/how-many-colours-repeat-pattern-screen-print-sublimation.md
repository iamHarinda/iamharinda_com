Not every printing method handles colour the same way, and the method you're planning to use should shape the design before it's finalized, not get discovered as a constraint after the artwork's already done and everyone's happy with it.

## Screen printing means fewer colours, by design

Screen printing works by pushing ink through a separate stencil, or screen, for each individual colour in the design. That means every additional colour adds real cost and production complexity, since each one needs its own screen and its own pass through the press. Screen-printed designs typically stay limited to somewhere between one and six colours, specifically because of this cost structure, and patterns designed for screen printing generally need to work within that limitation from the very start, using flat, distinct colour areas rather than gradients or subtle blending that screen printing just can't reproduce.

## Sublimation means effectively unlimited colour

Sublimation printing works completely differently. It's a dye-transfer process that can reproduce full-colour, photo-realistic designs with gradients, shading, and essentially unlimited colours, since it's not building up ink layer by layer through separate screens at all. This is why sublimation is the standard choice for the kind of complex, all-over, richly coloured patterns increasingly common on golf and performance apparel, designs that would be prohibitively expensive or simply impossible with screen printing's colour-by-colour cost structure.

## Embroidery is a completely different constraint

Embroidery isn't printing at all. It's thread, physically stitched in place, and it has its own limitations that have nothing to do with ink. Very fine detail and tiny text don't translate well into stitching. And while embroidery can technically use many thread colours, each one adds real machine time and cost to the job. Embroidered logos are typically simplified compared to a printed version of the same design, specifically to work within what stitching can accurately reproduce on fabric.

## Why this needs deciding before design starts, not after

A pattern designed freely with rich gradients and dozens of subtle colour variations, intended for screen printing, either needs a real redesign to reduce it to a workable colour count, or needs to switch to sublimation to be reproduced the way it was originally designed. Knowing your production method upfront lets a designer build the pattern correctly the first time, instead of discovering a mismatch after the design's already been approved and everyone's moved on.

## What to tell a designer before you order anything

Simply stating the intended production method, this will be screen printed, this is going through sublimation, this needs to work as an embroidered logo, is enough for a designer to make the right calls on colour count and detail level from the very first concept, instead of building something that needs reworking once it hits an actual production quote.

## A cost tip worth knowing

If a screen-printed design is creeping past six colours, ask your designer whether a couple of similar shades can merge into one without hurting the look. It's often a small compromise that saves a real amount of money on every single unit produced.

Tell your designer the production method up front and the design gets built to actually work with it from day one.
