Every wedding photographer runs into the same problem eventually: a getting-ready room lit by window light and warm bulbs, or a reception with string lights, uplighting and a phone flash all in one frame. A single white balance slider can't fix light that isn't actually one colour temperature — which is why these shots are the ones that most often come back from a quick edit looking slightly wrong.

## Why one white balance setting can't fix mixed light

White balance in Lightroom shifts the entire image toward or away from blue/yellow and green/magenta. That works when the whole frame is lit by one light source. When a frame has a window (daylight, ~5500K) and a lamp (tungsten, ~2700K) both contributing light, no single setting is correct for both — pick a setting that fixes the window and the lamp-lit side goes orange; fix the lamp side and the window light goes blue.

## Step 1: pick the light source that matters most

Usually that's whichever light is falling on skin, since skin tone accuracy matters more than background colour accuracy. Set white balance based on a neutral reference on the subject — not on the background window or the lamp itself.

## Step 2: isolate the mismatched area with a mask

Once the subject's skin reads correctly, the background light source is often now visibly wrong (too blue, too orange). This is where a luminance or colour range mask, or a simple background mask, earns its place:

- Mask the background separately and nudge its temperature back toward neutral without touching the now-correct subject.
- For a window in the frame, a graduated or radial mask lets you cool or warm just that region.

## Step 3: watch for green or magenta casts, not just blue/yellow

Fluorescent and some LED lighting introduces a green cast that the basic Temp slider doesn't fully correct — that's what the Tint slider is for. A shot that still looks slightly sickly after fixing Temp usually needs a Tint adjustment, not more Temp.

## Step 4: check skin against a true reference, not against the room

The room lighting can look correct on screen while skin still reads off, because human eyes adapt to ambient colour casts faster than a photo does. Compare skin tone against a genuinely neutral reference — a colour-calibrated monitor is what makes this judgment reliable in the first place; on an uncalibrated screen, "correct" skin tone can look different depending on the display.

## When to stop chasing perfect and mask by zone

Some frames — three or four light sources overlapping, a dance floor lit by a moving DJ light — aren't worth chasing to a single "correct" white balance. At that point, masking by zone (subject, background, specific light sources) and correcting each toward neutral independently is faster and more accurate than trying to find one global setting that compromises everywhere at once.
