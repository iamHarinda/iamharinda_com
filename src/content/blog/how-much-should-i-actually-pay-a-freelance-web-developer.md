Ask this question in any small business forum and you'll get answers ranging from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands, all confidently stated as the correct number. The truth is that both answers can be right, depending entirely on what's actually being built, and knowing where your own project falls on that range is what actually matters.

## Current hourly rate bands for 2026

Freelance web developers in the US typically charge between fifty and a hundred fifty dollars an hour, with a median sitting around eighty five. Junior developers doing simpler template-based work and small fixes tend to run twenty five to fifty dollars an hour. Mid-level developers building standard business sites average around seventy dollars an hour. Senior developers handling custom applications, performance optimization, and complex integrations charge ninety to a hundred eighty dollars an hour or more.

## What most small businesses actually spend on a full project

Most US small businesses spend somewhere between two thousand and eight thousand dollars on a freelance-built website in 2026. A straightforward five to seven page brochure site with a contact form, basic SEO setup, and a content system you can edit yourself typically lands at the lower end of that range. Adding e-commerce, booking functionality, membership logins, or a fully custom design pushes the cost toward the upper end and sometimes well beyond it.

## Why the range is this wide, and what actually explains it

The same words, "I need a website," describe wildly different projects depending on scope. A single landing page and a custom web application with a client login system both technically qualify as "a website," but they represent vastly different amounts of actual work, which is exactly why a specific quote requires a specific, detailed conversation rather than a generic number pulled from an online forum.

## Red flags in pricing that are worth noticing

A quote dramatically below the ranges above, for genuinely comparable scope, sometimes signals someone new to freelancing still calibrating their own rates, which isn't automatically bad but is worth factoring into your expectations around experience and reliability. A quote dramatically above the range for a simple project, with no clear justification for the premium, is worth questioning directly rather than assuming higher automatically means better.

## What actually affects the price beyond the base rate

Timeline pressure, a rush project condensed into a much shorter window than normal, typically costs more than the same scope on a relaxed timeline. Ongoing maintenance needs, whether you want a support arrangement after launch, adds a separate recurring cost worth budgeting for from the start rather than discovering later. Content readiness, whether you're providing finished copy and images or need help creating them, shifts both timeline and cost meaningfully.

## How to get an actually accurate number for your own project

Rather than asking "what's a fair price for a website," describe your specific project, page count, required features, whether you'll manage content yourself, to a few different developers and compare the quotes you actually receive. This grounds the pricing question in your real project instead of a generic range that might not reflect what you specifically need built.

## What ongoing costs to budget for beyond the initial build

Beyond the upfront project cost, factor in hosting, typically a modest monthly or annual fee, a domain renewal, and either your own time or a maintenance arrangement for updates and occasional fixes after launch. A quote that only covers the build itself is telling you half the real financial picture, and asking directly about these ongoing costs before signing anything avoids an unpleasant surprise once the invoice for the actual build has already been paid.

## Why the cheapest quote isn't automatically the smartest choice

A quote significantly below the market range for comparable scope sometimes means shortcuts, no real testing, a generic template stretched to fit, no ownership transfer of the code, that cost more in the long run than the upfront savings were worth. Comparing what's actually included, not just the final number, matters more than chasing the lowest bid available.

## How location and experience level shift these numbers further

Developers based in different regions, or working through different platforms, freelance marketplaces versus direct referral versus an established local business, can quote noticeably different rates for comparable work. Neither end of that spectrum is automatically wrong, but it's worth understanding that the number you see first depends partly on where and how you're sourcing candidates, not purely on the objective value of the work itself.

## What a fixed-price quote protects you from that hourly billing doesn't

Hourly billing puts the risk of scope creep or slower-than-expected progress on you as the client, since the meter keeps running regardless of how the project actually unfolds. A fixed-price quote, agreed after a proper scope conversation, shifts that risk onto the developer instead, which is exactly why a detailed upfront conversation matters so much before a price gets locked in either way.

## A simple way to sanity-check any quote you receive

Ask directly what's included in the number, design, development, testing across devices, a set number of revision rounds, and what would trigger an additional charge beyond it. A vague quote with no clear boundary around scope is far riskier than a higher number with everything clearly spelled out from the very start.
