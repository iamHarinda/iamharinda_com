It's a detail that's easy to overlook until it matters: receiving a finished design file doesn't automatically mean you're legally clear to sell products using it. Commercial use rights — what you're actually permitted to do with a design once it's delivered — are a real, separate consideration from the design work itself.

## What "commercial use" actually means

Commercial use rights are the specific permissions granted alongside a design that determine whether you can use it to make money — selling products featuring it, using it in advertising, applying it across a product line, and so on — as opposed to using it purely for personal, non-commercial purposes.

## Why this can be a real problem with the wrong source

Stock design elements, purchased clip art, or motifs pulled from generic pattern marketplaces sometimes come with limited licences — personal use only, a cap on how many units you can sell, or a requirement to purchase an extended licence separately for commercial or large-scale use. A design built from elements under a restrictive licence can leave you technically not permitted to actually sell the products you're making with it, even though you paid for the design.

## What custom, commissioned design work should include

When a design is created specifically for you — original artwork built from scratch for your brief, not assembled from someone else's licensed stock elements — commercial use rights should be included as standard, without a separate licence fee or usage cap. You're not licensing someone else's pre-made asset; you're commissioning original work made for your specific use.

## What to explicitly confirm before you order

- Is the design being built from scratch, or does it incorporate any pre-made stock elements that might carry their own licensing terms?
- Does the price include full commercial use rights, with no cap on units produced or sold?
- Can the design be used across multiple products, or is it licensed for one specific use only?
- Who retains the underlying rights to the design — do you have exclusive use, or could the same or a similar design be sold to someone else?

## Why this matters more as your order gets bigger

For a single small batch of shirts, licensing ambiguity is a manageable risk. For a full product line, a large production run, or a design that's central to your brand, unclear commercial rights are a real liability — worth explicitly confirming in writing before committing to a large order, not assumed based on having paid for the design.
