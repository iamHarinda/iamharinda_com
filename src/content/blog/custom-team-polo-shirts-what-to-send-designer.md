Ordering a custom polo design for a team, club, or company goes faster and lands closer to right on the first attempt when your brief is specific. None of what's needed here requires any design skill at all, just a bit of preparation before you reach out.

## Your logo or existing branding

If you have a logo, send the actual source file if you have one, Ai, EPS, SVG, or at minimum a high-resolution PNG, rather than a small image pulled off a website or screenshotted from somewhere random. A low-resolution logo limits what a designer can actually do with it cleanly, no matter how skilled they are.

## The garment colour, or colours

Design that's approved against white can look completely different once it's placed on the actual garment colour you're using. If you already know the polo will be navy, forest green, or a specific team colour, say so upfront. It changes how the design should get built, not just how it's finally applied at the end.

## Where the design actually goes

Left chest only? Chest and back? A sleeve detail too? Being specific about placement, even roughly, shapes how the design gets built. A design meant for a small chest placement gets approached differently than one meant to be a large back-panel graphic covering more space.

## The production method, if you already know it

Screen print, embroidery, or sublimation, if you already know which one you're using. If you don't know yet, that's fine too, just say so, and mention it as soon as you do decide, since it can genuinely affect how the design comes together.

## Any reference designs you like

Two or three polo designs, even from other brands or teams, that you like the feel of, with a note on what specifically appeals to you, the boldness, the colour palette, how minimal or busy it feels overall. This communicates far more precisely than a written description ever could on its own.

## Quantity and whether multiple colourways are needed

If the design needs to work across several garment colours, a team with home and away colours, for example, mention that upfront too. It's a different scope than a single-colourway design, and it's better to know going in.

## What a complete brief actually looks like

"We're a golf club with navy and white as our colours. Logo attached as a vector file. We want it on the left chest, small, and a larger version of a simplified motif on the back. This will be embroidered on the chest and screen printed on the back. We like the feel of these two references, bold but not too busy."

That's everything a designer needs to start with an accurate first concept instead of guessing at half the details.

## Why this preparation actually pays off

A vague brief means more revision rounds just to land somewhere close to what you pictured. A specific one, even a rough one, usually gets you there in one or two rounds instead of five.

Have your logo and a rough idea ready? Send it over and get concepts back with a realistic mockup on the actual garment.
