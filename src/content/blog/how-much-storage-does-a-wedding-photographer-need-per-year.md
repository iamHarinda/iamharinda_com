Storage planning tends to happen reactively, buying another drive the moment the current one fills up, rather than being calculated ahead of time based on actual booking volume. That reactive approach works until it doesn't, usually at the worst possible moment, mid-season, with a drive full and a wedding happening this weekend.

## The math that actually matters

Start with your average RAW file output per wedding, commonly fifty to eighty gigabytes depending on shoot length and how aggressively you cull in-camera. Multiply that by your expected bookings for the season. A photographer averaging sixty gigabytes per wedding across thirty bookings needs roughly one point eight terabytes just for that season's original files, before counting anything else.

## What else eats into that number

Edited exports add meaningfully on top of RAW originals, since a full-resolution delivered gallery, even compressed, adds real file size across hundreds of images. Backup copies, if you're following a proper multi-copy backup strategy, effectively multiply your total storage need by however many local copies you keep, on top of whatever lives in cloud storage separately.

## Why "buy more than you think you need" is the right instinct here

Storage needs only grow as a business grows, more bookings, higher resolution camera bodies over time, more retained years of past client work. Buying capacity with real headroom, rather than the exact minimum calculated for this season alone, avoids the scramble of an emergency purchase mid-season when a drive fills up unexpectedly during your busiest month.

## How resolution upgrades quietly change this math

Upgrading to a higher-megapixel camera body increases RAW file size meaningfully, sometimes by fifty percent or more compared to a previous body, which means storage calculations based on last year's file sizes can undersell what a new camera actually needs. Recalculating this whenever you upgrade gear, rather than assuming last year's numbers still apply, prevents a nasty surprise.

## What to do with older client galleries over time

Not every business needs to keep full-resolution RAW files indefinitely for every client from every year. A common approach: keep full RAW files for a defined window, a year or two, then either archive final edited selects only for older galleries or move full archives to cheaper, slower cold storage specifically meant for long-term retention rather than active daily access.

## Cloud storage costs at real volume

Cloud backup pricing scales with how much you're storing, and a photographer with several years of full-resolution galleries can find the monthly cost climbing meaningfully if every single file from every year stays in active cloud storage indefinitely. Reviewing what actually needs to stay in expensive, fast cloud storage versus what can move to cheaper archival tiers keeps ongoing costs proportional to what you're actually using it for.

## Planning storage purchases around your business calendar

Buying additional storage capacity during a slower off-season, rather than in a panic during peak wedding months, gives you time to research options properly and avoid paying a premium for whatever's immediately available when you're desperate. Treating storage as an annual planning line item, alongside gear and insurance, rather than a reactive emergency purchase, keeps the whole system running smoothly.

## A simple planning habit worth adopting

Once a year, ideally during your slowest stretch, calculate your actual storage usage from the past twelve months and project forward based on expected bookings for the year ahead. This turns storage from a recurring emergency into a predictable, budgeted part of running the business.

## A note on how camera format changes the baseline

Shooting compressed RAW versus uncompressed RAW, or a smaller sensor versus a full-frame body, changes average file size meaningfully, sometimes by a significant margin per image. Basing your storage math on your own actual camera and settings, rather than a generic average pulled from somewhere online, gives a far more accurate number to plan around.

## Accounting for second shooters in the total

If you regularly work with a second shooter, their files add to the total storage picture even if you're not the one directly responsible for backing them up. Factor their typical output into your planning if you end up storing or archiving their files alongside your own, since forgetting this piece is a common reason a seemingly generous storage calculation still comes up short by the end of a busy season.

## What happens if you underestimate and run out mid-season

Running out of storage mid-wedding, or mid-season, forces rushed decisions, deleting files that shouldn't be deleted yet, delaying an offload because there's nowhere to put it, or scrambling to buy an expensive drive at retail price with no time to shop around. None of these are decisions you want to make under pressure, which is exactly why calculating ahead of time and buying with genuine headroom matters more than it might seem worth the effort in a calm moment.

## Why this planning habit gets easier every year you do it

The first year of calculating actual storage needs is the hardest, since you're often estimating without much real data to work from. Every subsequent year gets easier and more accurate, because you're refining an existing estimate against real numbers from the year before rather than guessing from scratch each time a new season starts.

## A simple spreadsheet that keeps this manageable

A basic spreadsheet tracking bookings, average file size per wedding, and running storage totals, updated a few times a season, turns this from a vague worry into a number you can actually see coming. It takes minutes to set up and pays for itself the first time it prevents a mid-season storage crisis.
