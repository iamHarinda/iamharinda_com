If you've never ordered a custom polo or golf shirt design before, not knowing what the process actually involves is completely normal and nothing to feel awkward about. Here's a realistic walk-through, start to finish, so there are no surprises waiting for you along the way once you actually begin.

## Step one, an initial message before ordering

A quick description of what you need, the garment, the brand or team, roughly what you're picturing in your head at this early stage, lets a designer confirm it's actually a good fit before you commit to anything formally. This is also the moment to mention if you're unsure whether you need pattern design or something else entirely, like technical patternmaking, which is a different service handled by different specialists.

## Step two, sending your brief

Your logo or existing branding, if you have any at this point, garment colour, placement, production method if you know it already, and any reference designs you like from anywhere. The more specific this is, the closer the first concept lands to what you actually want, covered in more detail in a dedicated post on briefing a design well elsewhere on this blog.

## Step three, initial concepts

Depending on the package you've chosen, you'll typically get somewhere between one and five initial concepts, each shown with a realistic front and back mockup on the actual garment, not just flat artwork floating on a blank background somewhere disconnected from the real product.

## Step four, revisions

This is normal and expected, not a sign anything went wrong with the initial concept. You review the concept or concepts presented, note what's working and what isn't quite right yet, and revisions continue until the design is genuinely right and everyone's satisfied. On a well-structured service, revisions are unlimited and included in the price, with no extra charge tacked on per round of feedback.

## Step five, final approval

Once you're happy with the design and the mockup, it gets finalized, locked in as the version that'll be delivered in production-ready files ready to hand off to whoever's running the actual physical production.

## Step six, file delivery

The finished package, an editable vector source, Ai, a vector PDF, and flattened PNG and JPG versions, all at print resolution and cleared for commercial use, the formats a print shop, embroiderer, or supplier can actually work with directly without needing any additional conversion or rework on their end.

## Step seven, taking it to production

From here, the files go to whoever's handling actual production, a print shop, an embroidery service, a garment supplier you've already sourced. This is a separate step from the design itself. A pattern designer typically hands off print-ready files rather than running the physical production side of the business personally.

## What a realistic timeline actually looks like

For a straightforward polo design with clear reference material provided upfront, one to two days is a reasonable expectation for the first concepts, with revisions continuing at whatever pace suits the back-and-forth communication between both sides until it's fully approved and ready to move forward.

## What happens if you need to pause partway through

Life happens, and sometimes a project needs to pause mid-process, waiting on budget approval, waiting on a decision from a larger group, waiting on a garment supplier to confirm availability. A reasonable designer can hold a project at whatever stage it's reached without penalty, picking back up whenever you're ready to continue rather than requiring you to start over from scratch.

## How this process compares across different designers

While the general shape of this process, brief, concepts, revisions, final files, is fairly standard across the industry, the details vary, how many concepts are included, how many revision rounds, turnaround time for each stage. Comparing these specifics across a few options before committing helps set realistic expectations and avoids confusion partway through a project about what was actually promised at the very start of the relationship.

## What to keep from this process for your own records

Once a project wraps up, hold onto the full email or message thread alongside the final files, since it documents exactly what was agreed at each stage and can be genuinely useful reference material for a future reorder or a related project down the line with the same or a different designer.

## A final note for first-timers going through this for the first time

Nobody expects you to know design terminology walking into this process for the first time. A good designer will ask the right questions and fill in the gaps as needed. Your job is mostly just being clear about what you're picturing and honest about what you like or don't when concepts come back for your review.

## Why understanding this process reduces first-time anxiety

Ordering any kind of custom creative work for the first time carries a bit of natural uncertainty, will it turn out right, will the process feel confusing, will communication be smooth. Knowing the actual steps involved in advance, as laid out here, replaces that uncertainty with a clear, predictable roadmap you can follow confidently from your very first message through to receiving finished, production-ready files.

## Bringing it all together

From that very first message to holding print-ready files in hand, the whole process is built around clear steps and honest communication at each stage, not guesswork on either side of the relationship.
