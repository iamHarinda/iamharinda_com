If you've never ordered a custom polo or golf shirt design before, not knowing what the process actually involves is completely normal. Here's a realistic walk-through, start to finish, so there are no surprises waiting for you along the way.

## Step one, an initial message before ordering

A quick description of what you need, the garment, the brand or team, roughly what you're picturing in your head, lets a designer confirm it's actually a good fit before you commit to anything. This is also the moment to mention if you're unsure whether you need pattern design or something else entirely, like technical patternmaking, which is a different service handled by different people.

## Step two, sending your brief

Your logo or existing branding, if you have any, garment colour, placement, production method if you know it, and any reference designs you like. The more specific this is, the closer the first concept lands to what you actually want, covered in more detail in a dedicated post on briefing a design well elsewhere on this blog.

## Step three, initial concepts

Depending on the package, you'll typically get somewhere between one and five initial concepts, each shown with a realistic front and back mockup on the actual garment, not just flat artwork floating on a blank background somewhere.

## Step four, revisions

This is normal and expected, not a sign anything went wrong. You review the concept or concepts, note what's working and what isn't quite right, and revisions continue until the design is genuinely right. On a well-structured service, revisions are unlimited and included in the price, with no extra charge tacked on per round.

## Step five, final approval

Once you're happy with the design and the mockup, it gets finalized, locked in as the version that'll be delivered in production-ready files ready to hand off.

## Step six, file delivery

The finished package, an editable vector source, Ai, a vector PDF, and flattened PNG and JPG versions, all at print resolution and cleared for commercial use, the formats a print shop, embroiderer, or supplier can actually work with directly without any extra steps.

## Step seven, taking it to production

From here, the files go to whoever's handling actual production, a print shop, an embroidery service, a garment supplier. This is a separate step from the design itself. A pattern designer typically hands off print-ready files rather than running the physical production side of things.

## What a realistic timeline actually looks like

For a straightforward polo design with clear reference material, one to two days is a reasonable expectation for the first concepts, with revisions continuing at whatever pace suits the back-and-forth communication until it's fully approved.

## A final note for first-timers

Nobody expects you to know design terminology walking into this. A good designer will ask the right questions and fill in the gaps. Your job is mostly just being clear about what you're picturing and honest about what you like or don't when concepts come back.

Ready to see what this looks like for your own design? Message first with what you're picturing, no commitment required at that stage.
