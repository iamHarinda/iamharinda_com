publishDate: 2027-01-17
---

It's a reasonable assumption that a finished design is a finished design, regardless of how it eventually gets applied to the garment it's headed for. In practice, embroidery and sublimation printing are different enough as physical processes that the same artwork often needs adapting, sometimes significantly, to actually work well in each one of these very different production methods.

## Embroidery deals with real physical limits

Embroidery builds an image out of stitched thread, which gives it real physical constraints a printed image just doesn't have to deal with in the same way. Fine detail and small text get lost below a certain size no matter how good the machine doing the stitching actually is. Stitches simply can't render intricate detail cleanly, so a logo with fine linework or small text often needs simplifying specifically for embroidery to hold up once it's actually produced. Colour count matters for cost and complexity too. Every thread colour is a separate step in the digitizing and stitching process, so embroidered designs are typically simplified in colour compared to a full-colour printed version of the same design sitting next to it for comparison.

Gradients and fine shading don't translate at all into stitched thread. Embroidery works in solid stitched colour areas, so smooth gradients or subtle shading in a printed design generally need converting into distinct colour blocks before embroidery can handle them properly. And a separate digitizing step is always required for this production method. The artwork gets converted into a stitch file, a set of machine instructions, which is a distinct skill from the original design work itself. A design that looks great as artwork can still digitize poorly if it wasn't built with embroidery's limits in mind from the very beginning of the process.

## Sublimation stays much closer to what you designed

Sublimation dye-transfer printing reproduces full-colour, photo-realistic artwork, including gradients, fine detail, and complex colour blending, far more faithfully than embroidery ever manages to achieve. Sublimation is much closer to "what you design digitally is what appears on the garment," which is exactly why it's the standard choice for complex, all-over patterned designs that need to hold up in real detail once printed.

## What this means practically for your order

If a design needs to appear in both forms, say an embroidered chest logo and a sublimation-printed back panel on the same garment, it often makes sense to design two coordinated versions rather than forcing one version to work for both methods at once. A simplified version built specifically for embroidery, and a fuller, more detailed version for the sublimation-printed area sitting elsewhere on the same shirt entirely.

## What to actually tell a designer before ordering

Specify the production method for each placement on the garment, not just once for the whole order as a blanket statement covering everything. "Chest logo will be embroidered, back design will be sublimation printed" gives a designer everything needed to build each element correctly for its actual production method, rather than one version that ends up compromising for both and doing neither particularly well as a result.

## A quick real-world example worth picturing

A logo with a subtle drop shadow and fine text might look great sublimation printed on a back panel, but that same logo embroidered small on a chest would probably lose the shadow entirely and blur the tiny text into an unreadable mess. Two versions, same logo, same brand, built specifically for what each method can actually deliver on the finished product.

## A cost consideration worth knowing upfront

Embroidery generally costs more per unit for small runs but holds up better to repeated washing over years of wear. Sublimation tends to be more cost-effective for larger runs and complex, full-colour designs, but the finish can feel different to the touch compared to embroidered thread. Factoring in both design fit and this cost tradeoff together, rather than choosing a method purely on appearance, gives a more complete picture before committing to either option.

## A third option worth mentioning briefly

Heat transfer vinyl, a third common method, sits somewhere between the two in terms of design flexibility, handling moderate detail and a reasonable colour range at a lower cost than sublimation for smaller runs, though it doesn't hold up quite as well to heavy, repeated washing over several years compared to embroidery or proper sublimation. It's worth asking about as an option if budget is tight and the design doesn't need the full colour range sublimation offers.

## Why asking about method early saves real frustration later

Photographers and business owners alike sometimes assume the production method is a detail to sort out after the design is finished and approved. In practice, treating it as one of the very first questions, right alongside colours and placement, prevents the frustrating experience of loving a design on screen only to learn it needs significant rework once a specific production method gets locked in later in the process, after real time has already been invested in a version that doesn't quite fit.

Different placements, different production methods, the design gets built to actually work for each one from the start.
