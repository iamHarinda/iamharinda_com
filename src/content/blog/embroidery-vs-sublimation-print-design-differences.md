It's a reasonable assumption that a finished design is a finished design, regardless of how it eventually gets applied to the garment. In practice, embroidery and sublimation printing are different enough as physical processes that the same artwork often needs adapting, sometimes significantly, to actually work well in each one.

## Embroidery deals with real physical limits

Embroidery builds an image out of stitched thread, which gives it real physical constraints a printed image just doesn't have to deal with. Fine detail and small text get lost below a certain size. Stitches simply can't render intricate detail cleanly, so a logo with fine linework or small text often needs simplifying specifically for embroidery to hold up. Colour count matters for cost and complexity too. Every thread colour is a separate step in the digitizing and stitching process, so embroidered designs are typically simplified in colour compared to a full-colour printed version of the same design sitting next to it.

Gradients and fine shading don't translate at all. Embroidery works in solid stitched colour areas, so smooth gradients or subtle shading in a printed design generally need converting into distinct colour blocks before embroidery can handle them. And a separate digitizing step is always required. The artwork gets converted into a stitch file, a set of machine instructions, which is a distinct skill from the original design work. A design that looks great as artwork can still digitize poorly if it wasn't built with embroidery's limits in mind from the start.

## Sublimation stays much closer to what you designed

Sublimation dye-transfer printing reproduces full-colour, photo-realistic artwork, including gradients, fine detail, and complex colour blending, far more faithfully than embroidery ever manages. Sublimation is much closer to "what you design digitally is what appears on the garment," which is exactly why it's the standard choice for complex, all-over patterned designs that need to hold up in detail.

## What this means practically for your order

If a design needs to appear in both forms, say an embroidered chest logo and a sublimation-printed back panel, it often makes sense to design two coordinated versions rather than forcing one version to work for both methods at once. A simplified version built specifically for embroidery, and a fuller, more detailed version for the sublimation-printed area sitting elsewhere on the same shirt.

## What to actually tell a designer

Specify the production method for each placement on the garment, not just once for the whole order as a blanket statement. "Chest logo will be embroidered, back design will be sublimation printed" gives a designer everything needed to build each element correctly for its actual production method, rather than one version that ends up compromising for both and doing neither particularly well.

## A quick real-world example

A logo with a subtle drop shadow and fine text might look great sublimation printed on a back panel, but that same logo embroidered small on a chest would probably lose the shadow entirely and blur the tiny text into nothing. Two versions, same logo, same brand, built for what each method can actually deliver.

Different placements, different production methods, the design gets built to actually work for each one from the start.
