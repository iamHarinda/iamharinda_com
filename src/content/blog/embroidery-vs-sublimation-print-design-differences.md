It's a reasonable assumption that a finished design is a finished design, regardless of how it eventually gets applied to the garment. In practice, embroidery and sublimation printing are different enough as physical processes that the same artwork often needs to be adapted — sometimes significantly — to work well in each.

## Embroidery: thread has physical limits

Embroidery builds an image from stitched thread, which means it has real physical constraints a printed image doesn't:

- **Fine detail and small text get lost.** Below a certain size, stitches simply can't render intricate detail cleanly — a logo with fine linework or small text often needs simplifying specifically for embroidery.
- **Colour count matters for cost and complexity.** Every thread colour is a separate step in the digitizing and stitching process; embroidered designs are typically simplified in colour compared to a full-colour printed version of the same design.
- **Gradients and fine shading don't translate.** Embroidery works in solid stitched colour areas — smooth gradients or subtle shading in a printed design generally need to be converted into distinct colour blocks for embroidery.
- **A separate "digitizing" step is required.** The artwork gets converted into a stitch file (a set of machine instructions), which is a distinct skill from the original design — a design that looks great as artwork can still digitize poorly if it wasn't built with embroidery's limits in mind.

## Sublimation: much closer to what you see is what you get

Sublimation dye-transfer printing reproduces full-colour, photo-realistic artwork — including gradients, fine detail, and complex colour blending — far more faithfully than embroidery. Sublimation is much closer to "what you design digitally is what appears on the garment," which is why it's the standard choice for complex, all-over patterned designs.

## What this means practically

If a design needs to appear in both forms — say, an embroidered chest logo and a sublimation-printed back panel — it often makes sense to design two coordinated versions rather than forcing one version to work for both methods: a simplified version built specifically for embroidery, and a fuller, more detailed version for the sublimation-printed area.

## What to tell a designer

Specify the production method for each placement on the garment, not just once for the whole order. "Chest logo will be embroidered, back design will be sublimation printed" gives a designer everything needed to build each element correctly for its actual production method, rather than one version that compromises for both.
