Ask a couple why they picked a particular photographer over another with similar pricing and similar coverage, and "I loved the look of their photos" comes up constantly. That look — a consistent, recognizable editing style — isn't an accident. It's a small number of deliberate, repeated decisions.

## A style is a set of decisions, not a filter

The instinct is to think of "style" as a single preset or preview filter that produces a look in one click. In practice, a real, durable style is a consistent set of choices made across every gallery:

- A consistent approach to white balance (slightly warm, neutral, slightly cool) — not a fixed number, but a consistent *relationship* to true neutral.
- A consistent contrast curve — punchy and high-contrast, or soft and low-contrast, applied the same way gallery after gallery.
- A consistent colour grading approach — which tones get pushed in shadows, midtones and highlights.
- A consistent retouching philosophy — how much skin smoothing, how much correction versus how much is left natural.

None of these are a single slider. They're a pattern that repeats because the same judgment gets applied every time.

## Why a purchased preset doesn't create a style on its own

A preset can approximate a style's starting point, but it can't make the judgment calls — how much a specific frame's white balance needs adjusting, whether this specific skin tone needs warming or not — that make a style hold up across genuinely different lighting conditions. Two photographers using the identical preset will still produce visibly different final galleries, because the judgment applied on top of the preset is what actually determines the final look.

## How to actually build one

1. **Pick 10–15 of your best past edits** — the ones that feel most like "you," across different lighting conditions (not all from one wedding).
2. **Write down what they have in common** — specific, not vague. Not "warm and moody" but "shadows pushed slightly teal, skin kept close to neutral, highlights lifted warm."
3. **Turn that into your own preset**, built from your own edits rather than purchased — a starting point that's actually close to your real style, not someone else's.
4. **Apply it as a starting point, then correct per image** — the preset gets you 70% of the way; the remaining 30% is the per-image judgment that keeps skin tones accurate under different lighting.

## Keeping it consistent as you scale or outsource

The risk in outsourcing editing, or bringing on a second editor, is losing the consistency that took time to build. The fix is the same reference material used to build the style in the first place: give any outside editor the 10–15 reference images and the written description, not just a folder of RAW files with no direction. A specific brief — "keep skin warm but not orange, shadows lean teal, whites should stay bright" — gets a much closer match than "edit these in a nice style," even from a skilled editor.
