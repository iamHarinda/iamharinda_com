"Unlimited revisions" reads great on a pricing page, right up until you're the one sending a fourth round of feedback and wondering if you've become an unreasonable client. This worry is common enough to be worth addressing directly, because the honest answer is more reassuring than most photographers expect.

## What "unlimited" actually means in practice

A genuine unlimited revision policy means exactly what it says: no cap, no extra fee, no awkward conversation once you pass some invisible threshold. Services that offer this build the cost of reasonable back-and-forth into their base pricing from the start, expecting that most galleries need at least one round and some need several before they're genuinely right.

## What a normal number of rounds actually looks like

Most galleries settle within one to three rounds once an editor understands your style, whether through clear references upfront or through the first round's feedback itself. A first gallery with a brand new editor commonly needs more back-and-forth than a fifth or sixth gallery with someone who now knows your preferences well.

## When more rounds are still completely reasonable

If each round of feedback is addressing genuinely new, specific issues, not it's still not right but I can't say why, more rounds are simply part of getting a colour-critical process right. Wedding photos matter enough, and stay around long enough, that taking the time to get skin tones and consistency genuinely correct is worth more than rushing to a stopping point that doesn't actually satisfy you.

## When it's worth pausing and reconsidering the approach

If feedback rounds keep circling the same issue without genuine progress, round four addressing the same skin tone concern round one raised, that's a sign the underlying direction needs resetting rather than another small tweak. At that point, a direct conversation, or fresh, more detailed reference material, often resolves things faster than another incremental revision.

## Why editors generally don't mind a few extra rounds

An editor who's built their pricing around unlimited revisions has already accounted for this in what they charge, and reasonable, specific feedback rounds are a normal part of the job they've priced for, not a favour you're asking for outside the agreement. Genuinely difficult or vague feedback that never lands anywhere concrete is a different situation, but specific, honest notes are exactly what the revision process exists to handle.

## A practical way to keep rounds productive

Bundle your feedback into one clear, complete note per round rather than sending a new small request every few minutes throughout the day. This keeps the process efficient for both sides and tends to produce faster overall turnaround than a scattered stream of individual, disconnected requests sent piecemeal.

## What genuinely crosses a reasonable line

Revisions that ask an editor to redo work based on your own indecision, approving something and then changing your mind days later with no new information, are a different category than revisions addressing an actual quality issue. Most services handle occasional indecision graciously, but it's worth being mindful of the difference between refining toward accuracy and simply not having made a decision yet.

## What to do if you're genuinely unsure whether you're asking too much

If you find yourself hesitating before sending another round of feedback, worried about becoming a difficult client, ask yourself whether the concern is specific and real, not it still doesn't feel right versus this exact area still has a colour issue. If it's specific and real, send it without hesitation. A legitimate service would rather fix a genuine issue now than have you quietly settle for a gallery you're not actually happy with.

## Why some galleries genuinely need more rounds than others

A gallery shot in especially tricky mixed lighting, or one where your style has recently shifted and your reference material hasn't caught up yet, reasonably needs more revision rounds than a straightforward, evenly-lit gallery matched against well-established references. Judging what's "normal" against your hardest galleries rather than your easiest ones sets an unfair, inaccurate benchmark for yourself.

## How to tell the difference between a style mismatch and a fixable issue

If revisions keep circling without progress despite specific feedback, it's worth asking honestly whether the issue is a technical one that's fixable, or a genuine style mismatch that no amount of revision will close. The first is worth continuing to work through. The second is worth a direct conversation about whether this particular editor is the right long-term fit for your specific aesthetic.
