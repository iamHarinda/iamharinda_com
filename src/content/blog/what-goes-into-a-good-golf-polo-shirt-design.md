A common mistake in briefing a polo shirt design is thinking of it as "put my logo on a polo" — a placement task rather than a design task. A logo that looks great on a business card or a website often doesn't automatically look right once it's placed on a collared shirt, and the difference between those two outcomes is what a good polo design actually accounts for.

## Placement that respects the garment's shape

A polo shirt isn't a flat rectangle — it has a collar, a placket (the buttoned front section), sleeve seams, and a shape that curves around the body. A logo or print placed without accounting for this can end up looking awkwardly positioned once the shirt is actually worn — too close to the collar, off-center relative to the placket, or scaled in a way that reads differently on the body than it did on a flat mockup.

## Scale that works at the actual size

A design that looks balanced as a large image on screen can look completely different scaled down to fit a chest placement, or scaled up to cover a back panel. Good polo design considers the actual physical size the artwork will appear at, not just how it looks in an oversized digital preview.

## Colour that works against the garment colour, not just on white

A design approved against a white or transparent background can shift completely once placed on a coloured garment — a logo that reads clearly on white might lose contrast entirely on navy, or clash with a specific team colour. Strong polo design gets checked against the actual garment colour (or colours, if it's being produced in multiple colourways) before it's finalized.

## Consistency across front and back

Most polo orders need both a front design (often smaller, a chest placement) and a back design (often larger, more prominent) that feel like they belong to the same garment — same colour palette, same overall style — rather than two disconnected graphics that happen to be on the same shirt.

## Production method baked in from the start

As covered elsewhere, whether the design is headed for embroidery, screen print, or sublimation genuinely changes what the artwork should look like — colour count, detail level, and how fine the linework can be. A design built without this in mind often needs real rework once it reaches a production quote.

## What a genuinely good polo design brief includes

The garment colour, the intended placement (chest, back, sleeve), the production method, and — critically — a realistic mockup showing the design on the actual shirt shape, not just floating on a flat background. That mockup step is what catches placement and scale problems before anything goes to a printer.
