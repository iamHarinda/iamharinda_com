Paying a deposit, sending over your content and logo, and then watching messages go unanswered for weeks is one of the more common horror stories that makes business owners nervous about hiring a freelance developer at all. It happens often enough to be a real risk worth planning for, not just a rare, freak occurrence.

## Why this happens more than it should

Freelancing has a low barrier to entry, and some people take on more projects than they can actually manage, or lose interest partway through, or run into a personal crisis that derails their availability with no warning given to clients depending on them. None of this makes it acceptable, but understanding it helps explain why prevention matters more than hoping it simply won't happen to you.

## The single most important protection: payment structure

Never pay the full project cost upfront. A reasonable structure ties payment to milestones, a deposit to begin, a payment at a design approval stage, a final payment at launch, so a developer who disappears mid-project has only been paid for work actually delivered up to that point, not for work that never materialized.

## What to check before hiring that reduces this risk significantly

A verifiable track record, real reviews spanning real time, not a suspiciously thin or recent profile, reduces this risk considerably. A clear, written contract or agreement, even a simple one, stating deliverables and timeline, gives you something concrete to point to if a dispute arises later. Responsive, professional communication during the sales conversation itself is a reasonable predictor of how communication will hold up once real work is underway.

## What to actually do if it happens to you

Document everything, every message, every payment, every deliverable received so far, since this becomes essential if you need to dispute a credit card charge or pursue any other recourse. Reach out through every channel you have, email, phone, any social profile, since a genuine emergency sometimes explains a gap in communication that resolves once you reach the person another way. If a reasonable amount of time passes with no response, treat the relationship as over and start looking for who can pick up where things left off.

## Recovering your files and access if a developer disappears

If you don't already have your own access to hosting, your domain registrar, and the site's code, this is exactly the situation where that gap becomes a real problem, not just a theoretical risk. A new developer can often work with whatever exists on the hosting account itself even without cooperation from the original developer, but recovering full access sometimes requires contacting your hosting provider or registrar directly to reclaim control.

## Why this is exactly why ownership matters from day one

This entire scenario is the strongest possible argument for insisting on domain and code ownership transferring to you at launch, or even incrementally during the project, rather than waiting until a final handover that might never actually happen. A developer confident in finishing the work has no reason to resist this request upfront.

## Preventing this before it ever starts

Ask directly, before hiring anyone, what happens to access and files if the relationship ends unexpectedly partway through. A legitimate developer answers this without hesitation, and the answer itself tells you a great deal about how seriously they take client protection, regardless of how good their portfolio looks on the surface.
