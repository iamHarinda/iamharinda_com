A lot of small business owners get by for years on an Instagram page and a Google Business listing. Then they reasonably ask themselves, do I actually need a website too? The honest answer is that social media and a website do two different jobs. Skipping the second one costs you something real, even if it's not obvious on a normal day.

## What social media is genuinely good at

Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok are built for discovery and quick engagement. Someone scrolling sees your work, likes a post, maybe sends you a message. For visual businesses especially, that's real value, and nothing about a website replaces it entirely.

## What it just can't do for you

A social media page is rented space. The platform decides what people see, when your account might get flagged or restricted, and how your content gets formatted. You can't control the layout, and you can't get found by someone searching Google for exactly what you do unless they already know your account exists in the first place. It also doesn't feel permanent. A page with no posts in six months reads as "maybe closed," even if you're still very much in business and just busy.

## What a website gives you that social just doesn't

You own it, full stop. No platform can suspend your account, tweak the algorithm overnight, or redesign the whole interface out from under you without warning. People can find you by searching instead of just scrolling past your content by chance. Someone typing "wedding photographer near me" into Google is looking for exactly what you do, right now, and that's a completely different kind of visitor than someone passively scrolling a feed for entertainment.

It also looks more credible, fairly or not. A business with only a social page can read as smaller or less established than one with an actual website, especially to a customer who's never worked with you before and has nothing else to go on. And it's where your Google Business listing actually points. A "Website" link that just leads to an Instagram profile is a small trust signal you're leaving sitting on the table for a competitor to pick up instead.

## What this looks like for two similar businesses

Picture two photographers with equally good work, similar pricing, and similar experience. One has only an Instagram page, updated occasionally. The other has a simple website with clear pricing, a contact form, and a handful of galleries, plus the same active Instagram. When a potential client searches online after getting a referral from a friend, the second photographer shows up in search results with a polished landing page, while the first relies entirely on the friend remembering the exact Instagram handle to pass along. Over a year of bookings, that gap in findability adds up to real, missed business, not just a theoretical disadvantage.

## When social media alone genuinely works fine

If you're purely referral-based, don't want new inbound customers you don't already know, or you're testing an idea before committing further money, social alone can genuinely be enough, at least for now. A website isn't mandatory for every business at every stage of growth, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest and unhelpful.

## The realistic middle ground

You don't need a huge site on day one. A handful of pages, what you do, who you are, how to reach you, covers most of what a first website needs to accomplish. It doesn't replace your social accounts either. It gives people who find you through search, word of mouth, or a business card a permanent, professional place to land that you actually control from top to bottom, with nobody else able to change the rules on you later.

## One more thing worth saying

A lot of business owners wait years to build a site because it feels like a big project. It doesn't have to be. Even a simple three or four page site, built once and left mostly alone, beats no website at all by a wide margin, and it can usually go live faster than most people expect once they actually start.

## Why algorithm changes make relying on social alone risky

Platforms change how content gets shown constantly, and not always in your favour. A business that built its entire following around organic reach on one platform can watch that reach quietly shrink over a year as the algorithm shifts toward paid promotion or a different type of content entirely. A website doesn't have this problem. Once it's live and findable, it keeps working the same way regardless of what any single platform decides to change next. That stability alone is worth something to a business that's been burned by a sudden drop in visibility they had no control over.

## What a website adds even for a heavily social-first business

Even businesses that get most of their actual bookings through Instagram or TikTok often benefit from a simple site as a landing point, somewhere to send a link in bio that actually converts, rather than hoping a potential customer scrolls through an entire feed to find pricing or contact details. A single well-built landing page can outperform a link-in-bio tool for exactly this reason, since it's built specifically around getting someone from curious to booked, not around generic profile navigation the platform designed for a completely different purpose entirely.

## The bottom line for a business just starting out

Neither option needs to come first in some fixed order. What matters is that whichever you build, social account or website, actually gets finished and kept current, rather than half-started and abandoned. A simple, complete website beats an ambitious one that never launches, and a modestly active social account beats a beautifully designed one that hasn't posted anything in months. Consistency across whichever channels you choose to maintain matters more in the long run than picking the theoretically perfect combination of tools on day one and then losing momentum trying to keep all of it running at once.
