It's an uncomfortable question to even think to ask: if you paid for your website, don't you automatically own it? Not always — and the moment this usually surfaces is exactly the wrong time to find out, when you're trying to switch developers or platforms and discover you can't take anything with you.

## The three things "ownership" actually covers

- **The domain.** Registered under your name and your account, or under the developer's? If it's theirs, you don't control your own web address, full stop — they could, in theory, hold it hostage or simply lose it.
- **The code.** For a custom-built site, do you have access to the actual source files, or does everything live only on the developer's account? Without the code, you can't move the site anywhere else, even if you own the domain.
- **The platform account.** If the site runs on a builder (Wix, Squarespace) or a hosting account, is it under your business's login, or the developer's personal one?

You can technically "own" a website in the sense that you paid for it, while not controlling any of these three things in practice — which means you don't actually control your own website.

## Why this happens

It's often not malicious — a freelancer sets everything up under their own accounts because it's faster during the build, and handover just never happens afterward. But it also happens because some agencies and platforms deliberately keep you dependent, since locked-in clients are easier to keep billing.

## What to check right now if you already have a website

1. Log into your domain registrar account — is the business's own name and email listed as the owner, not a developer's?
2. Ask whoever built your site for the source code or repository, if it's custom-built — a legitimate answer is either "here it is" or a clear explanation of what platform it's on and how to access your own account for it.
3. Confirm hosting is billed to an account you control, not a reseller markup through someone else's account you have no visibility into.

## What to insist on before your next website is built

Ownership of the domain, the code (or full account access if it's platform-based), and the hosting account should transfer to you as a normal part of launch — not something you have to request, negotiate, or pay extra for later. If a developer is hesitant to commit to that upfront, that's worth taking seriously before you sign anything.
