"Do you want a CMS?" is a question a lot of business owners get asked without ever getting a plain answer to what it actually means. A CMS (content management system) is simply a way to edit your website's content — text, images, blog posts, product listings — without needing to know how to code or touch the underlying files directly.

## What having a CMS actually looks like

You log into a private dashboard, click on a page or post, edit the text or swap an image the way you'd edit a document, and publish the change — live on your site within seconds, with no developer involved. WordPress's admin panel is the most familiar example; there are also lighter, more modern "headless" CMS options that plug into a custom-coded site the same way.

## When you genuinely need one

- **You'll be updating content regularly** — a blog, changing promotions, new products, updated hours or menus.
- **More than one person needs to make edits**, and you don't want every small change to go through a developer.
- **Your business changes often enough** that a static site would be out of date within months.

## When you probably don't

- **The site is mostly informational and rarely changes** — a portfolio, a simple "who we are and how to reach us" site.
- **Any update, however small, is fine to send to a developer** rather than doing yourself — some businesses genuinely prefer this, since it means one fewer system to learn.
- **You're prioritizing the fastest, cleanest possible site**, and a CMS would add complexity you don't need yet.

## The tradeoff nobody mentions upfront

A CMS adds real value if you'll use it, but it also adds a layer that needs maintaining — software updates, a login to keep secure, sometimes a small ongoing cost. A site without one is simpler and often faster to load, precisely because there's less running behind the scenes. Neither is objectively better; the right choice depends entirely on how often you expect to touch the content yourself.

## A middle-ground worth knowing about

Modern "headless" CMS setups let you get the editing convenience — log in, change text, publish — without tying the whole site to a heavier platform underneath. It's a reasonable way to get real editing ability without taking on everything that comes bundled with a full platform like WordPress.

## The question to actually ask when you're getting quoted

Not "do I need a CMS" in the abstract, but "how often will I realistically want to change something on this site myself, without asking someone else to do it?" That answer, more than anything else, determines whether a CMS is worth the added setup.
