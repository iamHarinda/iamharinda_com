Approving a design on a laptop screen and then waiting weeks for a full production run to arrive is a nerve-wracking gap for anyone who's never done it before. Screens and fabric render colour, scale, and detail differently enough that "it looked great on my monitor" doesn't always translate directly to "it looks great on the actual shirt."

## Why screens and fabric never match perfectly

Screens display colour through backlit light, glowing pixels producing colour. Fabric reflects light off dye or thread instead, a completely different physical process that renders colour differently, sometimes subtly, sometimes significantly depending on the specific colours and materials involved. This gap is normal and unavoidable, which is exactly why the steps below matter.

## The realistic mockup as your first real check

A genuinely realistic mockup, rendered onto an actual photograph or accurate 3D model of the specific garment and colour you're ordering, gets you much closer to a true prediction than a flat design file viewed in isolation. This is the single most important checkpoint before moving toward physical production, and it's worth insisting on rather than approving from flat artwork alone.

## Requesting a physical sample before a full run

For any order beyond a small handful of shirts, requesting one physical sample garment with the actual design applied, before committing to the full production quantity, is worth the small extra cost and time. Seeing and touching the real result catches issues no digital preview fully replicates, exact colour under real light, how the fabric actually takes the ink or thread, how it feels against skin.

## What actually causes a printed result to disappoint

Colour mode mismatches, RGB approved on screen without accounting for CMYK print conversion, are one of the most common causes of an unexpectedly different-looking result. Fabric colour interaction, a design tested only against a white background suddenly looking different against navy or forest green, is another common surprise worth checking specifically.

## Questions worth asking your designer or supplier directly

Has this design been checked specifically for the CMYK colour conversion needed for printing, not just how it looks in RGB on a screen? Is the mockup showing the actual garment colour I'm ordering, not a generic placeholder? Can I get a physical sample before committing to the full order quantity?

## Why rushing this step costs more than it saves

Skipping the sample step to save a few days on a tight timeline risks discovering a real problem only once an entire production run has already shipped, at which point fixing it means paying for a second run entirely. The few days a sample costs are cheap insurance against that considerably more expensive outcome.

## A reasonable way to build confidence over repeat orders

Once you've ordered from a specific designer and supplier combination successfully once, and confirmed the digital preview reliably matches the physical result, subsequent orders carry much less of this uncertainty. The extra caution matters most on a first order with any new combination of designer and production partner.

## What a designer experienced in production actually adds here

A designer who regularly works with real printers and embroiderers, not just other designers, tends to build files correctly for production from the start, catching potential CMYK or fabric-interaction issues before they ever become a problem you'd otherwise only discover much later, at the sample stage or worse.

## Keeping copies of approved samples for future reference

Once a sample is approved, keep it physically or photograph it clearly alongside notes on the exact colours and settings used. This becomes a genuinely useful reference point for reorders or related future projects, saving real time re-establishing what "correct" actually looked like the first time around, months or years later.

## Why smaller test batches make sense for uncertain orders

If you're not entirely confident in a design direction yet, ordering a small test batch of five or ten shirts before committing to a full run of fifty or a hundred limits your exposure to a disappointing result. This costs a bit more per unit at the small scale, but it's cheap insurance against a much larger, more expensive mistake if something doesn't translate the way you expected.

## What to do if a sample reveals a problem

Finding an issue at the sample stage isn't a failure of the process, it's the process doing exactly what it's meant to do. A colour that reads slightly off, a placement that needs nudging, these are far cheaper and faster to fix on a single sample garment than they would be discovered only after an entire production run has already been completed and shipped.

## How lighting affects your own judgment of a sample

Examine a physical sample under a few different lighting conditions, daylight near a window, indoor artificial light, before making a final judgment, since colour can genuinely look different depending on the light it's viewed under. A quick check across a couple of lighting environments avoids approving, or rejecting, a sample based on one potentially misleading lighting condition alone.

## Why this extra step builds confidence for future orders

Once you've gone through the sample process once with a specific designer and supplier, and confirmed the final physical result matched what was promised digitally, you gain real, earned confidence in that specific combination for future orders. This isn't a step you necessarily need to repeat every single time once that trust is established.
