There's a specific look that gives away over-retouched skin instantly — a plastic, waxy smoothness that reads as "filtered" even to people who can't say exactly why. It happens when retouching removes texture along with blemishes. The fix isn't to retouch less — it's to retouch differently.

## Why blur-based smoothing fails

The fastest way to "clean up" skin is a blur or a heavy frequency-separation blur pass across the whole face. It works, in the sense that blemishes disappear — but it also removes pores, fine lines and the natural variation that makes skin look like skin instead of a rendered surface. Viewed at print size or on a large screen, that flatness is what reads as fake.

## Frequency separation, done selectively

Frequency separation (splitting an image into a low-frequency layer for tone/colour and a high-frequency layer for texture) is the standard professional technique — but the value is in editing the two layers independently, not in blurring everything on the low-frequency layer and calling it done:

- **Low-frequency layer:** smooth out uneven skin tone, redness, and shadowing — the color and light of the skin, not its surface detail.
- **High-frequency layer:** remove only genuine blemishes (spots, stray hairs, temporary marks) using a small, precise healing brush — never a broad blur.

The texture (pores, fine lines) stays on the high-frequency layer untouched except where there's an actual blemish to remove.

## Dodge and burn instead of blur for evenness

Uneven skin tone — blotchiness, shine, shadow under the eyes — is often better handled with a soft dodge-and-burn pass (lightening and darkening in low opacity, non-destructively) than with smoothing. It corrects tone without touching texture at all, which is why it's a slower but more reliable technique for skin that needs to hold up in a large print.

## A practical checklist before calling a retouch finished

1. Zoom to 100% and check that pores and fine texture are still visible on skin, not blurred away.
2. Compare the retouched image next to the original at a normal viewing distance — over-retouching is often only obvious zoomed out, not zoomed in.
3. Check skin tone consistency across the face — dodge and burn should even things out, not shift the overall colour.
4. Confirm blemish removal only touched genuinely temporary marks, not permanent features (moles, scars a client hasn't asked to have removed).

## Why this matters more for wedding portraits than most photos

Wedding portraits get printed large, framed, and looked at closely for decades. A retouch that looks fine on a phone screen can look obviously artificial on a 16x20 print on a wall. Retouching that keeps real texture ages better than retouching that chases a flawless, smoothed-out look.
