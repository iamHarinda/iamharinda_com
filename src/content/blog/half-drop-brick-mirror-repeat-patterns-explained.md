If a designer asks whether you want a straight repeat, a half-drop, or a brick repeat for your pattern, and you don't have a design background, that's a completely reasonable thing to not know offhand. Each one is just a different way of arranging how the tile repeats, and the choice genuinely changes how the finished pattern looks and feels once it's actually printed.

## Straight, or block, repeat

The simplest option. The tile repeats directly below and beside itself, in a straightforward grid, no offset at all. Rows and columns line up exactly. This tends to look clean and structured, but it can risk feeling a bit rigid or obviously "gridded" if the motifs get spaced too evenly or sized too large relative to the tile itself.

## Half-drop repeat

Every other column shifts down by half the tile's height, so the pattern staggers instead of lining up in a strict grid. This is one of the most common repeat types in fabric and apparel design specifically because it breaks up the obvious grid feel of a straight repeat, making the pattern feel more organic and less mechanically repetitive, even though it's built from the exact same single tile underneath it all.

## Brick repeat

A similar idea to half-drop, but the offset happens row by row instead, horizontally, like the staggered joints in a brick wall. It creates a similar organic, less-gridded feel to half-drop, but with a slightly different visual rhythm depending on the actual shape of the motifs involved.

## Mirror repeat

Alternating tiles get flipped, horizontally, vertically, or both, rather than simply repeated as-is over and over. This works particularly well for symmetrical or directional motifs. A mirrored repeat can make a pattern feel more balanced and less like the same exact shape marching across the fabric in one direction the whole way through.

## Which one should you actually pick

There's no universally "best" option here. It depends on your motifs and the feeling you're going for. Geometric, structured motifs often work well as a straight or brick repeat, since the underlying grid feel actually matches the style you're already going for. Organic, hand-drawn, or floral motifs usually benefit from a half-drop or mirror repeat, since it hides the mechanical grid and feels more natural sitting on fabric. Directional motifs, anything with an obvious "up," a golf club, an animal facing one way, often need a mirror repeat to avoid every single instance pointing the same direction in a way that starts to look odd once it's repeated dozens of times across a whole shirt.

## What to do if you're genuinely not sure

This is exactly the kind of decision worth leaving to a designer's judgment once they understand your motifs and the feel you want. You don't need to specify a repeat type in your brief at all, just describe the mood, structured and clean, or organic and flowing, and the right repeat type follows naturally from that description.
