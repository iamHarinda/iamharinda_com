If a designer asks whether you want a straight repeat, a half-drop, or a brick repeat for your pattern, and you don't have a design background, that's a completely reasonable thing to not know offhand without ever having worked in this field before. Each one is just a different way of arranging how the tile repeats, and the choice genuinely changes how the finished pattern looks and feels once it's actually printed across a real product.

## Straight, or block, repeat

The simplest option available to a designer. The tile repeats directly below and beside itself, in a straightforward grid, no offset at all between rows or columns. Rows and columns line up exactly with each other in a predictable arrangement. This tends to look clean and structured, but it can risk feeling a bit rigid or obviously "gridded" if the motifs get spaced too evenly or sized too large relative to the tile itself, drawing attention to the mechanical repetition rather than the design.

## Half-drop repeat

Every other column shifts down by half the tile's height, so the pattern staggers instead of lining up in a strict grid formation. This is one of the most common repeat types in fabric and apparel design specifically because it breaks up the obvious grid feel of a straight repeat, making the pattern feel more organic and less mechanically repetitive, even though it's built from the exact same single tile underneath the whole arrangement.

## Brick repeat

A similar idea to half-drop, but the offset happens row by row instead, horizontally, like the staggered joints you'd see in a brick wall. It creates a similar organic, less-gridded feel to half-drop, but with a slightly different visual rhythm depending on the actual shape of the motifs involved and how they interact with neighbouring tiles.

## Mirror repeat

Alternating tiles get flipped, horizontally, vertically, or both directions at once, rather than simply repeated as-is over and over across the surface. This works particularly well for symmetrical or directional motifs that would otherwise look repetitive marching in one direction. A mirrored repeat can make a pattern feel more balanced and less like the same exact shape marching across the fabric in one direction the whole way through a large surface.

## Which one should you actually pick

There's no universally "best" option here, since it depends entirely on your motifs and the feeling you're going for with the finished design. Geometric, structured motifs often work well as a straight or brick repeat, since the underlying grid feel actually matches the style you're already going for with that kind of artwork. Organic, hand-drawn, or floral motifs usually benefit from a half-drop or mirror repeat, since it hides the mechanical grid and feels more natural sitting on fabric or a garment surface. Directional motifs, anything with an obvious "up," a golf club, an animal facing one way, often need a mirror repeat to avoid every single instance pointing the same direction in a way that starts to look odd once it's repeated dozens of times across a whole shirt.

## What to do if you're genuinely not sure

This is exactly the kind of decision worth leaving to a designer's judgment once they understand your motifs and the feel you want for the finished product. You don't need to specify a repeat type in your brief at all, just describe the mood, structured and clean, or organic and flowing, and the right repeat type follows naturally from that description without you needing the technical vocabulary yourself.

## A practical way to compare options before deciding

If you're torn between two repeat types, ask your designer to show a quick preview of both on the same motif before committing to one direction. Seeing the actual visual difference side by side, rather than just reading a description, usually makes the right choice obvious in a way words alone can't fully convey to someone without design training.
