If a designer asks whether you want a "straight repeat," a "half-drop," or a "brick repeat" for your pattern, and you don't have a design background, that's a completely reasonable thing to not know offhand. Each one is just a different way of arranging how the tile repeats — and the choice genuinely changes how the finished pattern looks and feels.

## Straight (block) repeat

The simplest option: the tile repeats directly below and beside itself, in a straightforward grid. Rows and columns line up exactly. This tends to look clean and structured, but can risk looking a bit rigid or obviously "gridded" if the motifs are spaced too evenly or are too large relative to the tile.

## Half-drop repeat

Every other column is shifted down by half the tile's height, so the pattern staggers rather than lining up in a strict grid. This is one of the most common repeat types in fabric and apparel design specifically because it breaks up the obvious grid feel of a straight repeat, making the pattern feel more organic and less mechanically repetitive, even though it's built from the exact same tile.

## Brick repeat

Similar idea to half-drop, but the offset happens row by row (horizontally) instead of column by column — like the staggered joints in a brick wall. It creates a similar organic, less-gridded feel to half-drop, but with a slightly different visual rhythm depending on the shape of the motifs.

## Mirror repeat

Alternating tiles are flipped (mirrored) horizontally, vertically, or both, rather than simply repeated as-is. This works particularly well for symmetrical or directional motifs — a mirrored repeat can make a pattern feel more balanced and less like the same exact shape marching across the fabric in one direction.

## Which one to actually choose

There's no universally "best" option — it depends on the motifs themselves and the feeling you want:

- **Geometric, structured motifs** often work well as a straight or brick repeat, since the underlying grid feel matches the style.
- **Organic, hand-drawn, or floral motifs** usually benefit from a half-drop or mirror repeat, since it hides the mechanical grid and feels more natural.
- **Directional motifs** (anything with an obvious "up" — a golf club, an animal facing one way) often need a mirror repeat to avoid every instance pointing the same direction in a way that starts to look odd once repeated dozens of times.

## What to do if you're not sure

This is exactly the kind of decision worth leaving to a designer's judgment once they understand the motifs and the feel you're going for — you don't need to specify a repeat type in your brief, just describe the mood you want (structured and clean, or organic and flowing), and the right repeat type follows from that.
