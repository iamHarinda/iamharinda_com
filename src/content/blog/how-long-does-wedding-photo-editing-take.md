"When will I get my photos?" is one of the first questions a couple asks after the wedding, and one of the harder ones for a photographer to answer honestly — because the real answer depends on editing method, gallery size, and how much of the work is being done by hand versus by a bulk tool.

## What actually determines turnaround

- **Gallery size.** A 300-photo gallery and a 1,200-photo gallery aren't the same job, even with the exact same editing approach.
- **Editing method.** AI-assisted bulk correction can process a large gallery in hours; fully hand-edited galleries, image by image, take longer per photo but catch things a batch pass doesn't.
- **How much retouching is included.** Basic colour correction moves faster than detailed skin retouching or object removal on every image.
- **Season.** Peak wedding season (typically May through October in the US) means editors and photographers alike are working through backlogs, which stretches turnaround even when nothing about the individual job has changed.

## Realistic ranges

For a studio doing its own editing in-house, two to six weeks is a common range depending on season and backlog — some photographers state this upfront in their contract specifically to manage expectations. For an outsourced service pricing by volume with a dedicated editor rather than a shared queue, a gallery of 50–200 photos is often realistic in two to four days once the files are received, since it isn't competing with a photographer's other client work.

## Why a "sneak peek" gallery helps regardless of full turnaround

A large share of couples specifically want a small preview gallery within 48 hours of the wedding, even if they understand the full gallery will take longer. This isn't about rushing the full edit — it's a separate, smaller deliverable: 10–20 hero images edited quickly and well, while the full gallery continues on its normal timeline. It manages the anxious wait without compromising the complete edit.

## Setting a turnaround that's honest, not aspirational

The biggest source of client frustration isn't a longer turnaround — it's a turnaround promise that gets missed. A studio that says "four weeks" and delivers in three looks great; one that says "two weeks" and delivers in five looks unreliable, even if five weeks would have been reasonable to promise from the start. Whatever number goes in the contract should account for your actual backlog and season, not just the best-case editing time for a single gallery.

## If turnaround keeps slipping

If in-house editing consistently runs past the promised timeline, that's usually a volume problem, not a skill problem — the fix is either raising prices to reduce booking volume, or moving some or all of the editing to an outsourced service that isn't competing with shoot days and client calls for the same hours.
